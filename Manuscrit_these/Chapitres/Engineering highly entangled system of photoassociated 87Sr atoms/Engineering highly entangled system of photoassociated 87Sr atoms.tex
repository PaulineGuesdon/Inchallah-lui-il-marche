\label{chap 3}

While the previous chapter presented the individual evolution of atoms superposed in multiple states, this third chapter focuses on the loss processes through which two atoms 
are photoassociated to explore the molecular formation of $^{87}\text{Sr}$.
Photoassociation (PA) consists of illuminating colliding atoms at a specific frequency to form a bound molecular state. With the advent of cold and ultracold atoms, PA has 
become a high-resolution spectroscopic tool, as the extremely low initial kinetic energy significantly reduces thermal broadening and reveals subtle structures 
\cite{jones_ultracold_2006}.

In bosonic strontium, photoassociation near the narrow $^1\text{S}_0-{}^3\text{P}_1$
intercombination line has been studied extensively: the least-bound levels of the $0_u$ and
$1_u$ excited potentials of $^{88}\text{Sr}$ were measured \cite{zelevinsky_narrow_2006}, used to
demonstrate optical Feshbach resonances \cite{blatt_measurement_2011, blatt_systematic_2011},
and mass-scaled to $^{84}\text{Sr}$ and $^{86}\text{Sr}$ \cite{borkowski_mass_2014,
reschovsky_narrow-line_2018}; such a level was also used as the intermediate state to form
ground-state $^{84}\text{Sr}_2$ molecules by STIRAP \cite{stellmer_creation_2012}. The
ground-state potential $X^1\Sigma_g^+$ is known precisely, which gives the scattering lengths
of all isotopic combinations, including $^{87}\text{Sr}$ \cite{stein_1s1s_2010}. For the
fermionic isotope $^{87}\text{Sr}$, photoassociation has been reported on the broad
$^1\text{S}_0-{}^1\text{P}_1$ transition \cite{hill_photoassociative_2021}, whereas on the
intercombination line it has only been briefly mentioned. On this line, the hyperfine
structure of the $^3\text{P}_1$ state ($I=9/2$) splits each molecular level into many
components, which makes the spectrum dense and its assignment difficult, despite its promise
as a controllable interface for state manipulation.

Beyond fundamental spectroscopy, resolving these narrow molecular transitions is directly motivated by applications in quantum metrology. In this context, a fundamental 
benchmark for phase estimation with $N$ independent particles prepared in a coherent spin state is the Standard Quantum Limit (SQL), defined by the shot-noise scaling $\Delta\phi_{\text{SQL}} = 
1/\sqrt{N}$ \cite{pezze_quantum_2014}. For an ensemble of particles with effective spin $s$ where interferometry is performed on extreme projection states, this limit scales as $\Delta\phi_{\text{SQL}} = 
1/\sqrt{2Ns}$.

While standard protocols rely on coherent control, spin squeezing, or quantum gates to generate entangled states, the work of Foss-Feig et al. \cite{foss-feig_steady-state_2012} inspired our team to pursue 
an alternative route. Rather than viewing dissipation as a decoherence mechanism to be avoided, we aim to harness the two-body reactive losses associated with these narrow molecular potentials as a dissipative 
engine. As a long-term prospect, such a process can drive the system toward a stationary Dicke state in a zero-projection spin ($m = J_z = 0$). In this configuration, the collective spin variance scales 
quadratically ($\propto N^2$) rather than linearly as for uncorrelated states ($\propto N$). Consequently, the resulting phase sensitivity bypasses the standard $1/\sqrt{N}$ shot-noise scaling of the SQL and 
approaches the fundamental Heisenberg limit ($\Delta\phi \propto 1/N$), offering the ultimate precision allowed by quantum mechanics \cite{pezze_quantum_2014}.

To pave the way toward this goal, the work presented in this chapter focuses on an essential prerequisite: the spectroscopic identification of the molecular states involved in 
this spin-selective dissipative process. The main objective here is to benchmark the photoassociation mechanism itself, laying the necessary spectroscopic foundation for future
state-purification protocols.

This chapter opens with the fundamental concepts of scattering theory required to describe photoassociation and atomic density dynamics via one- and two-body loss processes. 
Next, we present preliminary experimental results obtained with $^{88}\text{Sr}$, taking advantage of its well-documented molecular potentials to calibrate and benchmark our 
experimental setup. Building upon these foundations, we then investigate the fermionic isotope $^{87}\text{Sr}$. We present the experimental spectra and discuss the technical 
challenges and systematic effects that could account for the weak losses observed on the molecular lines. Finally, we conclude with an analysis of the surprisingly rich line 
spectrum and line shapes, which suggest yet unexplained physical mechanisms at play.

\section{Introduction to photoassociation}
\subsection{Scattering theory}
Understanding the PA process necessitates an introduction to scattering theory to define key quantities such as the scattering length and the optical length. These parameters 
emerge naturally from the asymptotic 
analysis of the radial Schrödinger equation describing the relative motion of two colliding atoms.

In the study of inter-atomic interactions, the Born-Oppenheimer approximation provides a natural simplification. Due to the large mass ratio between the nuclei and the electrons,
 the nuclei move on a much slower timescale. One can therefore solve the electronic problem for fixed nuclear positions. The resulting electronic energy curve as a function of 
 the inter-nuclear distance $r$ defines the adiabatic potential $\hat{U}(r)$ experienced by the two atoms. 

Assuming a spherically symmetric potential $\hat{U}(r)$, the treatment of the two-body collision in the center-of-mass frame allows the total relative wavefunction to be 
factorized into radial and angular parts:
\begin{equation}
    \psi(r, \theta,\phi) = \frac{u_l(r)}{r} Y^m_l(\theta, \phi)
\end{equation}
where the spherical harmonics $Y^m_l(\theta, \phi)$ encode the angular dependence and $l$ denotes the partial wave quantum number. By introducing the reduced mass of the 
two-atom system $\mu_N = m_{\text{at}}/2$, 
the Schrödinger equation reduces to a one-dimensional radial equation for each partial wave $l$:

\begin{equation}
    \left( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu_N} \frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{\hbar^2 l(l+1)}{2\mu_N r^2} + \hat{U}(r) \right) u_l(r) = E u_l(r)
    \label{radial equation}
\end{equation}
E the eigenvalue associated to the eigenfunction $u(r)$.
This one-dimensional structure allows a direct analogy with the 1D quantum framework \cite{Landau}. To extract the scattering properties of the system, one studies the 
asymptotic behavior of the solutions at large 
distances. For $r\gg 1/k$, beyond the range of the inter-atomic potential, the general solution for $u_l(r)$ takes the form \cite{dalibard_interactions_nodate}:
\begin{equation}
    u_l(r) \xrightarrow{r\to\infty} a_l \sin\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}{2} + \delta_l\right)
    \label{solution at long r}
\end{equation}

where $a_l$ is a normalization factor, $k = \sqrt{2\mu_N E}/\hbar$ is the collision wavenumber, and $\delta_l$ is the phase shift acquired by the $l$-th partial wave due to the short-range interaction potential.

At low energy, $kb\ll1$ with $b$ the range of the potential (the distance
below which $\hat U(r)$ has a significant effect), the radial equation can be
solved separately in three regions: for $r\lesssim b$, where $\hat U$ dominates
over the energy, so that the solution does not depend on $k$; for
$b\ll r\ll1/k$, where only the centrifugal term remains and the solution is
$\alpha\,r^{l+1}+\beta\,r^{-l}$; and for $r\gg1/k$, where $k^2$ dominates and
one recovers Eq.~\ref{solution at long r}. Matching the solutions in the
overlap regions, the ratio $\beta/\alpha$ is set by the inner solution, hence
independent of $k$, while the matching with the outer solution gives
$\beta/\alpha\propto\tan\delta_l/k^{2l+1}$ \cite{dalibard_interactions_nodate}.
Therefore
\begin{equation}
    \tan\delta_l \propto k^{2l+1} \qquad (k\to0).
\end{equation}
This result requires a potential decaying fast enough at large distance
($-C_n/r^n$ with $n>2l+3$): for van der Waals ($n=6$), it holds only for
$l=0,1$ \cite{dalibard_interactions_nodate}.

For s-waves ($l=0$), this leads to the definition of the scattering length:
\begin{equation}
    a = -\lim_{k\to 0} \tan\delta_0/k
    \label{scattering length}
\end{equation}

The full wavefunction is recovered by summing over all partial waves. At large distances, this
superposition can be rewritten as an incoming plane wave and an outgoing scattered spherical wave:

\begin{equation}
    \Psi \xrightarrow{r\to\infty} e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot \mathbf{z}} + f(\theta) \frac{e^{ikr}}{r}
    \label{incoming outcoming scattering wave}
\end{equation}

where $f(\theta)$ is the scattering amplitude which can model both elastic and inelastic collisions and $\theta$ is the angle between the incident and scattered wavevectors. This amplitude expresses for a specific 
partial wave $l$ as
\begin{equation}
    f_l = \frac{1}{2 i k}(e^{2i\delta_l} -1)
\end{equation}

   \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{Chapitres/Engineering highly entangled system of photoassociated 87Sr atoms/images/Figures_pour_theorie/diffusion_sinus.pdf}
    \caption[Illustration of the amplitude's wavefunction of collisional particles]{The wavefunction $\Psi$ at large inter-atomic distances r behaves as a summation of an incoming plane wave
    and an outgoing spherical wave with attenuated amplitude $f(\theta)$. Here is a representation of this wavefunction for s-wave

    \label{fig:scattering_concept}

The assymptotical expression of $\Psi$ and $e^{ikz}$ is expressed in a spherical basis as a sum
over all the partial waves. Then we can give as in \ref{incoming outcoming scattering wave} a decomposition of $\Psi$:
\begin{equation}
    \Psi \simeq \frac{i}{2kr}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\left((-1)^l e^{-ikr} - S_l e^{ikr}\right)
    \label{1D schrodinger solution}
\end{equation}

where we identify a spherical part and a scattering term with $S_l = e^{2i\delta_l}$ a coefficient characterising the weight of the inelastic collisions.\\

In the presence of inelastic processes such as photoassociation, the colliding atom pair can be irreversibly lost
from the scattering channel-either by forming a bound molecular
state or by releasing kinetic energy. This loss of flux from the
incoming channel cannot be captured by a purely real phase shift,
as $|S_l|= 1$ implies full conservation of the wavefunction
amplitude. To account for this absorption of flux, $\delta_l$
must be extended to the complex plane, allowing $|S_l| < 1$
and introducing the possibility of inelastic scattering.\\

The associated elastic cross section representing the surface where the scope
of the potential is effective writes as
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{el} = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)|1-S_l|^2
    \label{sigma elastic}
\end{equation}
But we see in equation \ref{1D schrodinger solution} that $|S_l|$ needs to be $<1$ to allow dissipation of the
wavefunction. Defining $\delta_l$ as a complex number satisfies this condition and the inelastic cross section
is

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{inel} = \frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)(1-|S_l|^2)
    \label{sigma inelastic}
\end{equation}


Then from the condition $\delta_0 << 1$ we deduce an approximated scattering amplitude $S_0$
\begin{equation}
    S_0 = e^{2i\delta_0} \simeq 1 + 2i\delta_0
\end{equation}

Added to the result of \ref{scattering length},
\begin{equation}
     1 + 2i\delta_0 \simeq 1 - 2ika
\end{equation}

Because $\delta_0$ is complex, a direct result of these equations is that we can write a general scattering length as
\begin{equation}
    a = a_{bg} - i a''
\end{equation}
with $a''>0$.

At low temperatures we can re-express equations \ref{sigma elastic} and \ref{sigma inelastic} as
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{el} = 4\pi|a|^2
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{inel} = \frac{4\pi}{k}|a''|
    \label{sigma propto a}
\end{equation}

A typical example of elastic collision is that of two ground-state atoms, which can only
exchange kinetic energy. Photoassociation, in contrast, is an inelastic, dissipative
process: a laser couples two ground-state atoms to an excited molecular state, which then
decays, leading to the loss of the pair.


\subsection{PA and complex scattering length}
Photoassociation (PA) is a process in which a pair of colliding free atoms absorbs a photon to form a bound molecule in an excited electronic state. Effectively, because the short-lived molecule quickly decays into 
deeply bound states or dissociates with high kinetic energy, it escapes the optical trap. Consequently, the bound molecule is typically seen as lost, and the process is perceived as a macroscopic two-body loss effect.

In the atomic pair frame, the electric dipole interaction drives the system from the ground electronic state $\psi_g$ of potential $U_g(r)$ to the excited molecular state $\psi_e$ of potential $U_e(r)$. In the 
absence of a resonant laser, the internal evolution of the system is purely elastic and expresses as:
\begin{equation}
    i\hbar \frac{d}{dt}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \psi_g\\
        \psi_e
    \end{pmatrix}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2 + U_g & 0\\
        0 & -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2 + U_e - i\hbar \frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}
    \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \psi_g\\
        \psi_e
    \end{pmatrix}.
    \label{elastic evolution equation}
\end{equation}
Both $\psi_g$ and its field-free counterpart $\psi_g^{(0)}$ introduced below are
continuum states and cannot be normalized to unity. In what follows,
$\psi_g^{(0)}(r,E)$ denotes the s-wave reduced radial wavefunction
$u_0(r)$ of Eq.~\ref{radial equation} for the ground potential $U_g$ alone, at
collision energy $E=\hbar^2k^2/2\mu$. According to Eq.~\ref{solution at long r} with
$l=0$, it behaves at large distance as
$\psi_g^{(0)}(r,E)\to A_k\,\sin(kr+\delta_0^{\text{bg}})$, where
$\delta_0^{\text{bg}}$ is the real s-wave phase shift induced by $U_g$, related to the
background scattering length through Eq.~\ref{scattering length},
$\delta_0^{\text{bg}}\simeq -k\,a_{\text{bg}}$ at low energy, and $A_k$ is a
normalization constant. We adopt the standard energy normalization
\cite{bohn1997_prospects,jones_ultracold_2006}
$\langle\psi_g^{(0)}(E)\vert\psi_g^{(0)}(E')\rangle=\delta(E-E')$. The normalization
integral is dominated by the oscillating asymptotic region, where
$\int_0^\infty \sin(kr+\delta_0^{\text{bg}})\sin(k'r+\delta_0^{\text{bg}})\,dr
=\frac{\pi}{2}\,\delta(k-k')$. Since $\delta(k-k')=\frac{dE}{dk}\,\delta(E-E')
=\frac{\hbar^2k}{\mu}\,\delta(E-E')$, the condition reads
$A_k^2\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{\hbar^2 k}{\mu}=1$, which fixes
\begin{equation}
    \psi_g^{(0)}(r,E)\xrightarrow{r\to\infty}\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{\pi\hbar^2 k}}\,
    \sin\!\left(kr+\delta_0^{\text{bg}}\right).
    \label{psi_g0 energy normalized}
\end{equation}
The amplitude $A_k\propto 1/\sqrt{k}$ is the amplitude of the oscillations at large
distance, but the photoassociation overlap is set by the wavefunction at short
distance, near the Condon radius. There ($r\ll 1/k$), the sine can be expanded,
$\sin(kr+\delta_0^{\text{bg}})\simeq k\,(r-a_{\text{bg}})$, so that
$\psi_g^{(0)}(r,E)\simeq A_k\,k\,(r-a_{\text{bg}})$: the short-range amplitude is
$A_k\,k\propto k/\sqrt{k}=\sqrt{k}$. This $\sqrt{k}$ scaling of the short-range
wavefunction is the origin of the Wigner threshold law used below. This normalization also simplifies Fermi's Golden Rule: with
energy-normalized continuum states, the density of final states is equal to one,
so that the transition rates below can be written as $2\pi\vert\langle f\vert
H'\vert i\rangle\vert^2$, without an explicit density-of-states factor.

In the frame of \cite{nicholson_optical_2015} one treats the case when a laser is tuned near a specific vibrational level of the excited potential $U_e(r)$, coupling the free-atom continuum to the bound molecular 
state, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:PA_concept}.
\begin{figure}

    \caption[Scheme of the general concept of photoassociation]{Adapted from \cite{guttridge_photoassociation_2019}. General concept of photoassociation: a resonant laser couples a pair of colliding atoms from the free-atom continuum to a bound state of an excited 
    molecular potential. Within this potential, the molecule occupies quantized vibrational states, described by the solutions of the 1D radial Schrödinger equation plotted for each level.}
    \label{fig:PA_concept}
\end{figure}

This coupling introduces inelastic channels into the system. The effective coupled-channel equation becomes:
\begin{equation}
    i\hbar \frac{d}{dt}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \psi_g\\
        \psi_e
    \end{pmatrix}
    =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2 + U_g & \hbar\Omega/2\\
        \hbar\Omega/2 & -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\nabla^2 + U_e + \hbar \Delta -i\hbar\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}
    \end{pmatrix}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \psi_g\\
        \psi_e
    \end{pmatrix}
    \label{inelastic evolution equation}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ is the total decay linewidth of the excited molecular state (its radiative width $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$, defined below, plus possible non-radiative decay channels), $\Delta = \omega_e-\omega_L$ is the laser detuning from the excited bound state, and $\hbar \Omega/2$ represents the coherent 
optical coupling.

This matrix equation shows that the laser creates a bridge between the two manifolds. During a collision, two atoms approaching each other on the ground potential $U_g(r)$ have a non-zero probability to absorb a 
photon and transition to the excited molecular potential $U_e(r)$. Once populated, this state decays at the rate $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ (spontaneous emission at the rate $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}\leq\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$, plus possible non-radiative channels). To account for this particle loss, the molecular decay appears as an imaginary term $-i\hbar\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}$ in the Hamiltonian matrix.

The laser is detuned far from the atomic resonance, so that it does not excite single atoms: it is only resonant with the transition from the colliding pair to the bound molecular state. Since this free--bound coupling is weak, the excited molecular state remains weakly populated, and the pair spends most of its time on the ground-state potential.
To isolate the response of the excited molecular channel, we expand the closed-channel wavefunction $\psi_e(r)$ onto the bound vibrational eigenstates $\vert{}v_e\rangle$ of the excited Hamiltonian 
$\hat{H}_e = -\frac{\hbar^2\nabla^2}{2\mu} + U_e(r)$, which satisfy $\hat{H}_e \vert{}v_e\rangle = E_{v_e} \vert{}v_e\rangle$.

Following Bohn and Julienne \cite{bohn1997_prospects}, we keep in the expansion of
$\psi_e$ only the vibrational level $v_e$ closest to resonance (isolated-resonance
approximation). The other vibrational levels $v_e'$ also contribute to
$\langle\hat V\rangle$, but each of them is far from resonance and its contribution is
reduced by a large energy denominator. For $^{87}$Sr, the same argument applies to the
hyperfine levels $F'$ of $^3P_1$: their splitting ($\gtrsim1$~GHz
\cite{stellmerThesis}) is much larger than $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ and than the detuning
range explored here, so that each $(v_e,F')$ level can be treated as an isolated
resonance.

The coupled wavefunction $(\psi_g,\psi_e)$ always involves the excited molecular
state, but with a highly depleted amplitude, since the free--bound coupling is
weak. Projecting the second line of Eq.~\ref{inelastic evolution equation} onto
a specific isolated vibrational state $\vert{}v_e\rangle$ yields:
\begin{gather}
E \psi_e = \frac{\hbar \hat{\Omega}}{2}\psi_g + \left(\hat{H}_e + \hbar \Delta - i\hbar \frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2} \right)\psi_e\\
\bra{v_e}(E-\hat{H}_e - \hbar \Delta + i\hbar \frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2})\ket{\psi_e} = \bra{v_e}\frac{\hbar\hat{\Omega}}{2}\ket{\psi_g}
\end{gather}
We then assume that $\psi_e$ is dominated by this near-resonant level
(isolated-resonance approximation, see above), so that
\begin{equation}
\psi_e(r) \approx \frac{\left\langle v_e \middle| \frac{\hbar\hat{\Omega}(r)}{2} \middle| \psi_g \right\rangle}{E - E_{v_e} - \hbar\Delta + i\hbar\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}} v_e(r)
\end{equation}


Substituting this spatial projection back into the ground-state channel (first line of Eq.~\ref{inelastic evolution equation}) yields:
\begin{gather}
    i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\psi_g = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2\hat{\nabla}^2}{2\mu}+\hat{U}_g\right)\psi_g +\frac{\hbar\hat{\Omega}}{2}\psi_e\\
    \left(E+\frac{\hbar^2\hat{\nabla}^2}{2\mu} - \hat{U}_g\right) \psi_g = \frac{\hbar\hat{\Omega}}{2} \psi_e\\
    \left(E+\frac{\hbar^2\hat{\nabla}^2}{2\mu} - \hat{U}_g\right) \psi_g =  \underbrace{\frac{\frac{\hbar\hat{\Omega}}{2}\ket{v_e}\bra{v_e}\frac{\hbar\hat{\Omega}}{2}\ket{\psi_g}}{E-E_{v_e} - \hbar\Delta + 
    i\hbar\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}}}_{\displaystyle \equiv\ \hat{V}\psi_g}
\end{gather}
\begin{equation}
\left(-\frac{\hbar^2\hat{\nabla}^2}{2\mu} + \hat{U}_g(r) + \hat{V}\right)\psi_g(r) = E \psi_g(r)
\label{psi e dans psi g}
\end{equation}
One wants to isolate the term that concerns the ground state to observe the modifications of it from the coupling to the molecular state. Then by projecting $\ket{\psi_g}$ in \ref{psi e dans psi g} we define $\langle\hat{V}\rangle$ as:
\begin{equation}
\langle \psi_g | \hat{V} | \psi_g \rangle = \frac{\left| \left\langle v_e \middle| \frac{\hbar\hat{\Omega}(r)}{2} \middle| \psi_g \right\rangle \right|^2}{E - E_{v_e} - \hbar\Delta + 
i\hbar\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}}
\label{V_opt}
\end{equation}

\textbf{From $\hat V$ to a shift of the scattering length.}

The general relationship used here between a coupling potential and a
resulting shift of the scattering length is the same Green's function
argument that underlies the two-channel treatment of Feshbach resonances in
\cite{goral_adiabatic_2004} (see their Eqs.~13, 17, 19 and 22): there, the
coupling matrix element $\langle\phi_{\text{res}}\vert W\vert\phi_p^{(+)}
\rangle$ plays exactly the role of our $\langle v_e\vert\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}
\vert\psi_g^{(0)}\rangle$, and their scattering-length shift (their Eq.~22)
has the same structure as the one derived below. We reproduce this result
directly in position space, working at leading order in the laser coupling.

To relate $\langle\hat V\rangle$ to $a(\Delta)$, compare the full solution
$\psi_g$ of Eq.~\ref{psi e dans psi g} to the background solution
$\psi_g^{(0)}$ of the same equation with $\hat V\to0$, both evaluated at the
same threshold energy $E\to0$:
\begin{equation}
    -\frac{\hbar^2\hat\nabla^2}{2\mu}\psi_g^{(0)} + \hat U_g\,\psi_g^{(0)} = E\,\psi_g^{(0)},
    \label{psi_g0}
\end{equation}
which is simply Eq.~\ref{psi e dans psi g} with the coupling to the
molecular channel switched off, consistent with $\psi_g^{(0)}$ describing
purely elastic collisions on $U_g$ alone as in Eq.~\ref{elastic evolution equation}.
In this derivation only, the two radial wavefunctions are normalized at zero energy,
so that they behave as $r-a$ at large distance; the energy normalization of
Eq.~\ref{psi_g0 energy normalized} is restored just after. At $E=0$, the two radial
equations read $\psi_g^{(0)\prime\prime}=\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}U_g\,\psi_g^{(0)}$ and
$\psi_g^{\prime\prime}=\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}(U_g+\hat V)\,\psi_g$. Multiplying the first
one by $\psi_g$, the second one by $\psi_g^{(0)}$, and subtracting gives
$$\frac{d}{dr}\Big[\psi_g^{(0)\prime}\psi_g-\psi_g^{\prime}\psi_g^{(0)}\Big]
=\psi_g^{(0)\prime\prime}\psi_g-\psi_g^{\prime\prime}\psi_g^{(0)}
=-\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}\hat V\,\psi_g\,\psi_g^{(0)}.$$
Integrating from $r=0$, where both wavefunctions vanish, to $r\to\infty$,
where $\psi_g^{(0)}\to r-a_{\text{bg}}$ and $\psi_g\to r-a$ by definition of
the scattering length (so that the bracket tends to $a_{\text{bg}}-a$), yields
$$\delta a \equiv a-a_{\text{bg}} = +\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}
\int_0^\infty \hat V(r)\,\psi_g(r)\,\psi_g^{(0)}(r)\,dr.$$
As expected, a repulsive coupling ($\hat V>0$) increases the scattering length, as
for a hard sphere whose scattering length is its radius.
This integral still contains the full, unknown $\psi_g$, so nothing has been
solved yet. To make progress, write $\psi_g=\psi_g^{(0)}+\delta\psi$, where
$\delta\psi$ is the correction to the wavefunction caused by switching on
$\hat V$. Since $\hat V$ already involves the coupling $\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}$
twice -- once to excite to $v_e$, once to decay back -- it is proportional
to $\Omega^2$; the correction $\delta\psi$ it induces is therefore also of
order $\Omega^2$. Splitting the integral according to
$\psi_g=\psi_g^{(0)}+\delta\psi$ gives one term with $\psi_g^{(0)}$ in place
of $\psi_g$, plus an extra term proportional to $\hat V\,\delta\psi$; since
both factors are of order $\Omega^2$, this extra term is of order $\Omega^4$
-- a much smaller correction, which can be dropped consistently with the
weak-population assumption already stated above. Keeping only the leading
term thus amounts to replacing $\psi_g$ by $\psi_g^{(0)}$ inside the
integral:
$$\delta a \approx \frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}
\int_0^\infty \hat V(r)\,\left[\psi_g^{(0)}(r)\right]^2\,dr,$$
i.e. Eq.~\ref{V_opt} evaluated on the background threshold state.

We now return to the energy-normalized wavefunction of
Eq.~\ref{psi_g0 energy normalized}, which is the one entering the Fermi Golden Rule
below. The coupling $\hat V$ only acts at short distance, where the energy-normalized
wavefunction is $A_k\,k\,(r-a_{\text{bg}})$ with $A_k=\sqrt{2\mu/(\pi\hbar^2k)}$: it is
equal to the zero-energy-normalized one multiplied by $A_k\,k$. Replacing the latter by
the former therefore multiplies the integral by $1/(A_k k)^2=\pi\hbar^2/(2\mu k)$, and
\begin{equation}
    a(\Delta) = a_{\text{bg}} + \frac{\pi}{k}\,\langle\hat V\rangle
    = a_{\text{bg}} + \frac{\pi}{k}\,
    \frac{\left|\left\langle v_e\middle|\frac{\hbar\hat\Omega(r)}{2}\middle|\psi_g^{(0)}\right\rangle\right|^2}
    {E-E_{v_e}-\hbar\Delta+i\hbar\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}},
    \label{a_delta_intermediate}
\end{equation}
where $\psi_g^{(0)}$ is now energy-normalized.
\textbf{Explicit form of $l_{\text{opt}}$ via the Wigner threshold law.}

Following Bohn and Julienne \cite{bohn1997_prospects}, one can define the
stimulated rate $\Gamma_{\text{stim}}$ from $\ket{v_e}$ to $\ket{\psi_g^{(0)}}$
by the Fermi Golden Rule as
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{\text{stim}}(k) = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\left(\frac{2\pi I}{c}\right)d^2\left| \bra{v_e} \psi_g^{(0)} \rangle \right|^2
    \label{gamma_stim_def}
\end{equation}
with $I$ the laser intensity, $c$ the speed of light, and $d$ the transition
dipole moment; $\frac{\hbar\hat\Omega(r)}{2}=\vec d\cdot\vec E$ is the dipolar
coupling to the laser field $\vec E$, so that
$\left|\left\langle v_e\middle|\frac{\hbar\hat\Omega}{2}\middle|\psi_g^{(0)}\right\rangle\right|^2
=\left(\frac{2\pi I}{c}\right)d^2\left|\langle v_e\vert\psi_g^{(0)}\rangle\right|^2$: the
numerator of Eq.~\ref{a_delta_intermediate} is identified with
$\hbar\Gamma_{\text{stim}}(k)/2\pi$. $\Gamma_{\text{stim}}$ depends on the \textbf{Franck-Condon factor} $\langle v_e\vert\psi_g^{(0)}\rangle$,
the overlap between the (energy-normalized) ground-state scattering
wavefunction and the excited molecular state.

As shown after Eq.~\ref{psi_g0 energy normalized}, the amplitude of
$\psi_g^{(0)}$ at short distance, where it overlaps with $v_e$, scales as
$\sqrt{k}$. Writing $\psi_g^{(0)}(r,E\to0)\approx\sqrt{k}\,f_0(r)$ with $f_0$ a
$k$-independent function, one obtains
$\vert\langle v_e\vert\psi_g^{(0)}\rangle\vert^2=k\vert\langle v_e\vert
f_0\rangle\vert^2$, so that $\Gamma_{\text{stim}}(k)\propto k$. Writing this
proportionality as \cite{yan_controlling_2013}
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{\text{stim}}(k) = 2 k\,l_{\text{opt}}\,\Gamma_{\text{rad}}
    \label{eq:gamma_stim_blatt}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ is the radiative linewidth of the molecular state
($\gamma_m$ in \cite{blatt_systematic_2011}), equal to the total molecular linewidth
$\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ ($\gamma$ in \cite{blatt_systematic_2011}) when no non-radiative
decay (e.g.\ predissociation) broadens the line, and $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}\geq\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ otherwise.
This relation defines the $k$-independent \textbf{optical length}
\begin{equation}
    l_{\text{opt}} \equiv \frac{\pi}{\hbar\Gamma_{\text{rad}}}\left(\frac{2\pi I}{c}\right)d^2\left\vert\langle v_e\vert f_0\rangle\right\vert^2,
    \label{lopt_def}
\end{equation}
which is independent of collision energy and scales linearly with the laser
intensity $I$, as required. Throughout this chapter, $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ denotes
the \textit{radiative} (natural) width of the molecular state,
$\Gamma_{\text{rad}} \simeq 2\Gamma_{\text{at}} = 2\pi\times15$~kHz. The optical
length is always defined with respect to this radiative width, even when other
mechanisms broaden the observed line (see Sec.~\ref{sec:determining_lopt}), so
that it keeps its meaning of an intensity-dependent Franck--Condon factor. The
normalization by $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ rather than $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ is natural
since $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}\propto d^2$ is set by the same dipole moment as the coupling:
$d$ drops out (up to angular factors) and $l_{\text{opt}}\propto\lambda_a^3 I\,\vert\langle v_e\vert f_0\rangle\vert^2/c$,
as in \cite{blatt_systematic_2011}, whereas additional decay channels do not enter the coupling.

\textbf{Compact resonant form.}

Replacing the numerator of Eq.~\ref{a_delta_intermediate} by
$\hbar\Gamma_{\text{stim}}/2\pi=\hbar\,k\,l_{\text{opt}}\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/\pi$
(Eq.~\ref{eq:gamma_stim_blatt}), the factors $\pi$ and $k$ cancel and, after division
of the numerator and denominator by $\hbar$,
$$a-a_{\text{bg}}=\frac{l_{\text{opt}}\Gamma_{\text{rad}}}
{(E-E_{v_e})/\hbar-\Delta+i\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}}.$$
The quantity $(E-E_{v_e})/\hbar-\Delta$ is the detuning of the laser from the molecular
resonance, including the collision energy, counted positively on the blue side. From
now on, $\Delta$ denotes this detuning, $\Delta=\omega_L-\omega_{\text{res}}$, which is
the opposite sign convention from Eq.~\ref{inelastic evolution equation} and the one
used in the rest of this chapter. The scattering length then takes the compact
resonant form
\begin{equation}
a(\Delta) = a_{\text{bg}} + \frac{l_{\text{opt}}\Gamma_{\text{rad}}}{\Delta + i\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}}
\label{a(delta)}
\end{equation}
where $a_{\text{bg}}$ is the real background scattering length representing native elastic collisions \cite{yan_controlling_2013}.
This is the expression of \cite{blatt_systematic_2011} for an isolated resonance,
$\alpha=a_{\text{bg}}+\ell_{\text{opt}}\gamma_m/(\Delta+i\gamma/2)$, with $\gamma_m\to\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ and $\gamma\to\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$.

To distinguish how the laser independently alters the elastic interactions and the loss mechanisms, we multiply the resonant fraction in Eq.~\ref{a(delta)} by the complex conjugate of its denominator. This algebraic
 separation successfully decouples the scattering length into its real (dispersive) and imaginary (absorptive) components, $a(\Delta) = a_{\text{bg}} + \delta a(\Delta) - i a''(\Delta)$:
\begin{equation}
    a(\Delta) = a_{\text{bg}} + \frac{l_{\text{opt}}\Gamma_{\text{rad}}\Delta}{\Delta^2 + \left(\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}\right)^2} - i \frac{l_{\text{opt}}\Gamma_{\text{rad}}\frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}}
    {\Delta^2 + \left(\frac{ \Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{2}\right)^2}
\end{equation}
The real light-induced shift $\delta a(\Delta)$ governs the modification of the elastic properties of the gas, whereas the imaginary part $a''(\Delta)$ directly quantifies the rate of inelastic two-body losses.
\subsection{General scaling laws for PA}
\label{sec:scaling_laws}
To connect this microscopic scattering amplitude to observable experimental quantities, one must translate these individual collision events into a population decay that writes as 
\cite{martinez_de_escobar_two-photon_2008}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dN}{dt} = -\beta(T) N^2 - \Gamma N
\end{equation}
where $\beta(T)$ is related to the two-body loss rate $K_2$ and $\Gamma$ is the one-body loss rate that depends experimentally mostly on the vacuum quality.\\

In quantum scattering theory, the fundamental two-body loss rate coefficient $K_2(k)$ is directly driven by the absorptive (imaginary) part of the scattering length $a''$ derived from Eq.~\ref{V_opt}:
\begin{equation}
    K_2(k) = \frac{16\pi\hbar}{m_{\text{at}}} a''(k, \Delta)
    \label{eq:K2_a_second}
\end{equation}

Combined to Eq.~\ref{sigma propto a}, $K_2(k) = \frac{4\hbar k}{m_{\text{at}}}\sigma_{\text{inel}}$.
Eq.~\ref{eq:K2_a_second} holds in the linear regime $\delta_0\ll1$ used above. Keeping instead the
exact $S_0=(1-ika)/(1+ika)$, whose first-order expansion is the $1-2ika$ used above, one has
$1-|S_0|^2=4k\,a''/(1+2ka''+k^2|a|^2)$, with $a$ the complex scattering length of
Eq.~\ref{a(delta)}, so that Eq.~\ref{eq:K2_a_second} is multiplied by
$(1+2ka''+k^2|a|^2)^{-1}$. Inserting Eq.~\ref{a(delta)} (neglecting $a_{\text{bg}}$) and
$\Gamma_{\text{stim}}=2k\,l_{\text{opt}}\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$, this factor equals
$\left[\Delta^2+\Gamma_{\text{mol}}^2/4\right]/\left[\Delta^2+\frac14(\Gamma_{\text{mol}}+\Gamma_{\text{stim}})^2\right]$. 
One obtains the complete expression for
the loss coefficient as a function of the collision wavevector $k$, equivalent to
Eq.~(9) of Blatt et al. \cite{blatt_systematic_2011} (written over the common denominator $\gamma^2$) with $\gamma_m\to\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$,
$\gamma\to\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ and $\mu=m_{\text{at}}/2$:

\begin{equation}
    K_2(k) = \frac{4\pi\hbar}{m_{\text{at}}\,k}\,
    \frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}\,\Gamma_{\text{stim}}(k)}{\Delta^2+\frac14\big(\Gamma_{\text{mol}}+\Gamma_{\text{stim}}(k)\big)^2}
    = \frac{8\pi\hbar}{m_{\text{at}}}\,
    \frac{l_{\text{opt}}\,\Gamma_{\text{rad}}\,\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}{\Delta^2+\frac14\big(\Gamma_{\text{mol}}+\Gamma_{\text{stim}}(k)\big)^2}
    \label{K2 complet Blatt}
\end{equation}

where $m_{\text{at}}$ is the atomic mass, $\Delta = \omega_{\text{L}} - \omega_{\text{mol}}$ is the laser detuning from the molecular resonance (including the collision energy, as in Eq.~\ref{a(delta)}), and $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ is the total molecular linewidth.

While $K_2$ characterizes the fundamental two-body collision process, the experimentally observed decay coefficient $\beta$ depends on the spatial density distribution of the atomic cloud of width $\sigma_x, 
\sigma_y, \sigma_z$. The relation between $K_2$ and $\beta$ is derived by integrating the local loss over a Gaussian density profile:
\begin{gather}
\frac{dN(t,T)}{dt} = -K_2 \iiint n^2(x,y,z,t,T) \, d^3r \label{eq:3.24} \\[1.5ex]
\text{with } n(x,y,z,t,T) = n_0 \exp\left( -\frac{x^2}{2\sigma_x(T)^2} - \frac{y^2}{2\sigma_y(T)^2} - \frac{z^2}{2\sigma_z(T)^2} \right) \label{eq:3.25} \\[1.5ex]
\text{and } n_0 = \frac{N(t,T)}{(2\pi)^{3/2}\sigma_x(T)\sigma_y(T)\sigma_z(T)} \label{eq:3.25bis} \\[1.5ex]
\frac{dN(t,T)}{dt} = -K_2 \frac{N(t,T)^2}{2\sqrt{2}V_{\text{e}}(T)} \equiv -\beta(T) N(t,T)^2 \label{eq:3.26} \\[1.5ex]
\text{where } V_{\text{e}}(T) = (2\pi)^{3/2} \sigma_x(T) \sigma_y(T) \sigma_z(T) \label{eq:3.27} \\[1.5ex]
\text{Then } \beta(T) = \frac{K_2}{2\sqrt{2}V_{\text{e}}(T)} \label{eq:3.28}
\end{gather}
consistent with the definitions found in \cite{han_photoassociation_2018}.

For low temperatures, we neglect the contribution of the wavevector since $k \to 0$. In this threshold regime, the light-induced linewidth becomes negligible compared to the total molecular width 
($\Gamma_{\text{stim}}(k) \ll \Gamma_{\text{mol}}$). On resonance ($\Delta = 0$), substituting Eq.~\ref{eq:gamma_stim_blatt} into Eq.~\ref{K2 complet Blatt} allows the wavevector $k$ to cancel out from the 
prefactor, and the microscopic PA rate coefficient simplifies to:
\begin{equation}
    K_2 = \frac{16h}{m_{\text{at}}}\,l_{\text{opt}}\,\frac{\Gamma_{\text{rad}}}{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}}
    \label{K2 et lopt}
\end{equation}
which is equal to $K_2 = \frac{16h}{m_{\text{at}}}l_{\text{opt}}$ when $\Gamma_{\text{rad}} = \Gamma_{\text{mol}}$.
This fundamental result demonstrates that at low temperatures, the maximum photoassociation rate becomes independent of the temperature and scales purely linearly with the laser intensity $I$ through its dependence 
on $l_{\text{opt}}$. Structurally, $K_2$ is maximized when the ground- and excited-state wavefunctions exhibit maximum overlap at the Condon radius. In a classical picture, this distance corresponds to the outer turning point of the bound
molecular state, where the two atoms of the molecule spend most of their time, maximizing the
transition probability.

Eq.~\ref{K2 et lopt} is the relation through which $l_{\text{opt}}$ is defined
experimentally. It is however only valid at the \textit{peak} of an ideal line,
whose width is the radiative width $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ (i.e.\ $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}=\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$). Any additional broadening
(laser noise, thermal distribution, inhomogeneous light shift, extra decay
channels of the molecular state) lowers the peak value of $K_2$ while leaving
$l_{\text{opt}}$ unchanged; how to account for it is detailed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:determining_lopt}.

Finally, by combining Eq.~\ref{eq:3.28} and the low-temperature limit of $K_2$, we obtain the total macroscopic evolution equation of the trapped population:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dN}{dt} = -\frac{4\sqrt{2} h }{V_{\text{e}} m_{\text{at}}}l_{\text{opt}}N^2 - \Gamma N
\label{one and two body losses equation}
\end{equation}
which highlights that the global loss process is driven by the tunable optical length $l_{\text{opt}}$ and enhanced by the peak atomic density $n_0 \propto 1/V_{\text{e}}$. As Eq.~\ref{K2 et lopt}, this expression
assumes an unbroadened line probed at its center; in general, the two-body term
must be written with the measured loss coefficient $K_2$.

\subsection{Handling multiple molecular potentials}
So far, the photoassociation (PA) process has been presented assuming a simplified picture with a single excited-state molecular potential $U_e$. However, the molecules created via PA exhibit a rich internal 
structure, making the identification of accessible excited states essential for predicting and optimizing the loss rate coefficient $\beta(T)$.

\subsubsection{Angular momentum coupling and state enumeration}
Spin-orbit coupling plays a dominant role in shaping the excited molecular structure near the $^1\text{S}_0 - {}^3\text{P}_1$ intercombination asymptote. The total angular momentum of the molecule is defined by 
$T = R + F = R + I + J$, where $F = f_1 + f_2$ is the coupled atomic angular momentum, $J = j_1 + j_2$ is the total electronic angular momentum, and $R$ is the rotational angular momentum.

Along the internuclear axis, the projection of the electronic angular momentum is quantified by:
\begin{equation}
    \Omega = \Lambda + \Sigma
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda$ and $\Sigma$ represent the internuclear projections of the orbital and spin angular momenta, respectively. Because spin-orbit interaction is strong relative to rotational coupling at short to 
long ranges, the molecule is appropriately described within Hund's case (c) framework (see Fig.~\ref{fig:hund}), where $\Omega$ remains a good quantum number.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{Chapitres/Engineering highly entangled system of photoassociated 87Sr atoms/images/Figures_pour_theorie/hund_case_c.pdf}
    \caption[Hund's case (c) coupling scheme]{Hund's case (c) coupling scheme, where strong spin-orbit interaction couples the total electronic angular momentum to the internuclear axis with projection 
    $\hbar \Omega$. In most ultracold photoassociation scenarios, the rotational quantum number is low ($R = 0, 1$).}
    \label{fig:hund}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Effective hamiltonian and potential modeling}

Taking into account the hyperfine-induced mixing between $g$ and $u$ electronic manifolds ($0_u, 1_u, 0_g, 1_g$) at long range—analogous to observations in $^{173}\text{Yb}$ \cite{han_photoassociation_2018}—we 
estimate a total of 981 accessible $(R_f, T^f)$ molecular configurations. This massive density of states illustrates the extreme complexity of the excited molecular spectrum to be mapped experimentally.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Chapitres/Engineering highly entangled system of photoassociated 87Sr atoms/images/Figures_pour_theorie/ytterbium.pdf}
    \caption[Density of molecular potentials for $^{173}\text{Yb}$ \cite{han_photoassociation_2018}]{Density of molecular potentials for $^{173}\text{Yb}$ with three hyperfine states, adapted from \cite{han_photoassociation_2018}, giving rise to numerous triplets of quantum numbers $(T, F, R)$. 
    The spectral lines are identified as $\Omega_{u/g}$, where $\Omega$ is the total orbital momentum projection of the molecule and $u/g$ denotes the electronic parity (see Fig.~\ref{fig:u-g symmetry}).}
    \label{fig:pa-ytterbium-potentials}
\end{figure}

The high density of states expected for $^{87}\text{Sr}$ makes spectral assignment particularly challenging. To move beyond a statistical estimation and accurately predict the energy levels of these 981 configurations,
 precise modeling of the molecular interaction is required. This involves describing the coupling between the rotation of the nuclear frame, the electronic degrees of freedom, and the hyperfine structure.

Following the molecular picture introduced in Section~3.1.1, the energy of these states is governed by the total hamiltonian $\hat{H}$, which for a Sr-Sr system of reduced mass $\mu_N$ can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H} = \frac{p_r^2}{2\mu_N} + \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu_N r^2} R(R+1) + U_{BO} + \hat{H}_{\text{hf}}
    \label{eq:general_hamiltonian_molecule}
\end{equation}
the first and second terms representing the kinetic energy and radial energy of the molecule. Higher-order electronic interactions have been neglected depending on the relevant Hund's case. For $^{87}\text{Sr}$, 
the Born-Oppenheimer potential $\hat{U}_{\text{BO}}$ is approximated using a long-range expansion similar to $^{173}\text{Yb}$:
\begin{equation}
    U_{\text{BO}}(r) = -\frac{C_6}{r^6} \left( 1 - \frac{\sigma^6}{r^6} \right) - s \frac{C_3^{\Omega}}{r^3}
    \label{eq:sr_potential}
\end{equation}
where $s = +1$ and $s = -1$ correspond to $g$ and $u$ potentials, respectively. $C_6$ and $C_3^{\Omega}$ are Van der Waals and dipole-dipole coefficients \cite{borkowski_line_2009}, $\Omega$ still standing for 
projection of angular momentum, and $\sigma$ is a free parameter to adjust with the experimental results.

The atomic hyperfine Hamiltonian $\hat{H}_{\text{hf}}$ acts solely on the $^3\text{P}_1$ atom (with nuclear spin $i_1 = 9/2$ and electronic angular momentum $j_1 = 1$), as the ground-state $^1\text{S}_0$ atom has 
$j_2 = 0$ and thus no hyperfine interaction:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{H}_{\text{hf}} = A \, (\mathbf{i}_1 \cdot \mathbf{j}_1) + B \, \frac{3(\mathbf{i}_1 \cdot \mathbf{j}_1)^2 + \frac{3}{2}(\mathbf{i}_1 \cdot \mathbf{j}_1) - i_1(i_1+1) j_1(j_1+1)}{2 i_1 (2 i_1 - 1) \, 2 j_1 
    (2 j_1 - 1)}
\end{equation}
where $A$ and $B$ are the magnetic dipole and electric quadrupole hyperfine constants, respectively.

Among these potential curves, approximately 80 bound internal states were experimentally identified for $^{173}\text{Yb}$ \cite{han_photoassociation_2018}. Atom-loss spectra, recorded as a function of PA laser 
detuning, reveal the position of bound states relative to the atomic resonance $|^1\text{S}_0, F=5/2\rangle \to |^3\text{P}_1, F=7/2\rangle$. Near this threshold, atoms are illuminated for $30\,\text{ms}$ at low 
intensity ($I = 37\,\text{mW/cm}^2$) to avoid power broadening, resolving bound states closest to the dissociation limit. At larger red detunings, higher laser power and optimized exposure times are used to maximize
 signal depth.

Theoretically, these experimental loss lines are assigned by computing the eigenvalues of the effective Hamiltonian and adjusting parameters such as $C_n$ coefficients to match observed vibrational levels 
\cite{borkowski_line_2009}. Several semi-classical tools aid this assignment: the WKB approximation describes the near-threshold bound states, while LeRoy-Bernstein scaling predicts the spacing of consecutive 
vibrational lines near the dissociation limit without requiring detailed short-range potential data. Finally, mass scaling across strontium isotopes \cite{borkowski_mass_2014} serves as a robust benchmark to 
cross-check these energy assignments.


\subsection{Internal energy states}
\subsubsection{WKB approximation}

The Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin (WKB) approximation is a semi-classical method used to estimate
the vibrational energies of a diatomic system. Theoretical potentials alone are not accurate
enough to predict ab initio the position of a given level; however, once the position of one
bound state has been measured experimentally, WKB-based methods allow the positions of the
other levels to be predicted.

In this approach, the wavefunction is assumed to take the form of a modified plane wave of wavevector $k(x)$,
$\psi(x) = \frac{C}{\sqrt{k(x)}}e^{i\int_{x_0}^{x} k(x') dx'}$ \cite{sakurai2020modern}. These waves are solutions 
to the one-dimensional Schrödinger equation for two particles with reduced mass $\mu$ and 
eigenenergy $E$:

\begin{equation}
    -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + U(x)\psi = E\psi
    \label{eq:schrodinger_1d}
\end{equation}

This leads to the expression of the local wavevector $k(x)$:
\begin{equation}
    k(x) = \frac{p(x)}{\hbar} = \frac{\sqrt{2\mu(E-U(x))}}{\hbar}
\end{equation}

The validity of this approximation is governed by the condition $\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{d\lambda}{dx} \ll 1$, 
implying that the local de Broglie wavelength $\lambda$ must vary slowly compared to the scale of the 
atoms' motion. This can be explicitly rewritten in terms of the potential gradient:

\begin{equation}
    \left| \frac{\mu \hbar \frac{dU}{dx}}{\left[2\mu(E-U(x))\right]^{3/2}} \right| \ll 1
    \label{eq:wkb_validity}
\end{equation}

This expression shows where the semi-classical approach fails: near the classical
turning points $x_1$ and $x_2$ of Fig.~\ref{fig:pa-sakurai-wkb}, where $E \approx U(x)$, the
kinetic energy vanishes and the local wavelength diverges, so that the condition of
Eq.~\ref{eq:wkb_validity} is violated. Around these points, connection formulas, typically
using Airy functions, are required to match the solutions across the turning points.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Chapitres/Engineering highly entangled system of photoassociated 87Sr atoms/images/Figures_pour_theorie/WKB_sakurai.pdf}
    \caption[Illustration of the WKB semi-classical approximation in a simplified potential well]{Semi-classical (WKB) approximation in a simplified potential well
    $U(x)$. The energy level $E$ intersects the potential at the turning points $x_1$ and 
    $x_2$. In the classically allowed Region II, the wavefunction oscillates with an amplitude 
    proportional to $1/\sqrt{k(x)}$ where in the forbidden Regions I and III, the wavefunction is 
    evanescent.}
    \label{fig:pa-sakurai-wkb}
\end{figure}

At positions $x=x_1$ and $x_2$, the momentum reaches zero and becomes imaginary as the energy 
level $E$ crosses the potential $U(x)$. In the classically forbidden regions I and III, the 
wavefunction is evanescent and characterized by the decay constant $\kappa(x) = \sqrt{2\mu(U(x)-E)}/\hbar$. 
Around the turning points, it is standard to linearize the potential to connect the oscillatory 
and evanescent solutions.

To ensure a physical stationary state, the phase accumulated by the wave as it reflects between 
the barriers at $x_1$ and $x_2$ must satisfy a quantization condition. By ensuring the continuity 
of the solutions over $[0, +\infty[$, the phase integral must follow:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{x_1}^{x_2} k(x)dx = \left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi
    \label{eq:wkb_quantization}
\end{equation}

This formula, known as the Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition, enables the identification of 
the discrete energy eigenvalues $E_n$ that satisfy the integral. these values correspond to the 
vibrational states of the molecular potential.

\subsubsection{Leroy-Bernstein scaling}


The LeRoy-Bernstein scaling law \cite{leroy1970dissociation} builds upon the WKB approximation to identify the position of the 
high-lying vibrational states near the dissociation limit. This model is particularly powerful as 
it relies solely on the long-range behavior of the potential, bypassing the need for a detailed 
description of the complex short-range interactions. 

Near the dissociation limit $D_e$, occurring at large internuclear distances $r$, the potential 
can be approximated by its leading long-range term:
\begin{equation}
    U(r) \approx D_e - \frac{C_n}{r^n}
\end{equation}

where $C_n$ is the long-range coefficient (typically $n=6$ for Van der Waals or $n=3$ for resonant 
dipole-dipole interactions). For a vibrational state $\nu$ with energy $E_{\nu}$, we define the 
binding energy as $\epsilon_{\nu} = D_e - E_{\nu}$. Substituting this into the Bohr-Sommerfeld 
quantization condition (Eq. \ref{eq:wkb_quantization}), we seek solutions for:

\begin{equation}
    \int_{r_{min}}^{r_{max}} \frac{\sqrt{2\mu}}{\hbar} \sqrt{\frac{C_n}{r^n} - \epsilon_{\nu}} \, dr = (\nu_D - \nu) \pi
\end{equation}

where $\nu_D$ is a non-integer parameter representing the effective vibrational index at the 
dissociation limit. After integration, the relationship between the binding energy and the 
vibrational quantum number is given by:

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\nu} = \left[ (\nu_D - \nu) \frac{(n-2)\,\hbar\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2\mu}\,C_n^{1/n}} \frac{\Gamma(1+1/n)}{\Gamma(1/n + 1/2)} \right]^{\frac{2n}{n-2}}
\end{equation}

Here, $\Gamma$ denotes the Euler Gamma function. This expression is often rearranged to show the 
scaling of the vibrational spacing:
\begin{equation}
    E_{\nu} = D_e - \left[ (\nu_D - \nu) H_n \right]^{\frac{2n}{n-2}}
\end{equation}
\label{leroy-bernstein}
where $H_n$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    H_n = \frac{(n-2)\,\hbar\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{2\mu}\,C_n^{1/n}} \frac{\Gamma(1+1/n)}{\Gamma(1/n + 1/2)}
\end{equation}

This notation highlights that the binding energy follows a power law relative to the "distance" $\nu_D - \nu$
from the dissociation limit, where the exponent is purely determined by the nature of the long-range 
interaction.

\subsubsection{Mass scaling and isotopic shifts}

Mass scaling is a powerful method used to predict the vibrational energy levels of different isotopes 
from a single known potential. By adjusting the reduced mass $\mu$ corresponding to the isotope considered, 
one can determine the phase constraint on the vibrational states. While this approach neglects the hyperfine 
structure, it provides a reliable estimation of the spectral range where vibrational resonances are expected.

In particular, $^{88}Sr$ is a bosonic isotope with a nuclear spin $I=0$, meaning it possesses no hyperfine 
structure. Its photoassociation spectrum is significantly simpler than that of fermionic $^{173}Yb$ (see Fig. 
\ref{fig:pa-ytterbium-potentials}), involving only two accessible potentials in the first excited molecular 
state: $0_u$ and $1_u$. Based on the results presented in \cite{zelevinsky_narrow_2006}, the potential can be 
modeled as:
\begin{equation}
    U(r) \approx -\frac{C_{6}}{r^6} -\frac{C_3, \Lambda}{r^3} + \frac{\hbar^2 R(R+1)}{2\mu r^2}
\end{equation}

for the two molecular symmetries $\Lambda = 0, 1$. Since the Born-Oppenheimer approximation remains valid 
across the strontium isotopes ($^{84}Sr, ^{86}Sr, ^{88}Sr$), the electronic potential $V_{BO}(r)$ is assumed 
to be identical for all species. Consequently, only the reduced mass $\mu$ in the WKB phase integral varies:

\begin{equation}
    \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{\sqrt{2\mu(E-U(x))}}{\hbar} dx = \left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi
\end{equation}

The experimental positions of the first vibrational states for the $0_u^+$ potential in $^{84}Sr, ^{86}Sr$, 
and $^{88}Sr$ are shown as red crosses in Fig. \ref{fig:pa-mass-scaling}. These data points allow for the 
refinement of the potential shape $U(r)$. The fitted functions (blue lines) then enable the prediction of 
the frequency ranges where $^{87}Sr$ resonances should occur. Note that mass scaling applies
to the ground-state potential: Martinez de Escobar et al. determined the scattering lengths of
all isotopic combinations, including $^{87}$Sr--$^{87}$Sr \cite{martinez_de_escobar_two-photon_2008},
but they give none for the excited states. For $^{87}$Sr, the hyperfine structure of the excited
state prevents a direct mass scaling of the $^{88}$Sr levels.
Notably, discontinuities observed for the 
$^{84}$Sr isotope correspond to avoided crossings resulting from couplings with the potential correlated to the $^1S_0+{}^1D_2$ asymptote.

In conclusion, combining semi-classical WKB theory with LeRoy-Bernstein scaling offers a practical framework to estimate vibrational spacings near the intercombination threshold. This approach serves as a reliable 
guide for bosonic isotopes like $^{88}\text{Sr}$, presented in the next section. However, it cannot account for the $^{87}\text{Sr}$ photoassociation spectra, where instead of the dense manifold of lines, only a 
few narrow resonances are experimentally observed.

In this first part of the chapter, I have presented the theoretical picture of the photoassociation process where the following introduced material will help to understand the experimental results presented in the next section:
\begin{itemize}
    \item I demonstrated the relation of the two-body collision rate coefficient $K_2$ with the scattering length, which includes the parameter $l_{opt}$ caracterizing the strength of the inelastic loss process.
    \item I presented the different molecular potentials and their symmetries, which should help for future understanding of the results to identify the molecular lines of $^{87}Sr$.
    \item I presented two ways of estimating the position of the molecular states: the WKB approximation to determine the position of the vibrational states that could be complemented by the LeRoy-Bernstein scaling.
    The second one is the mass scaling that predicts -- without considering the hyperfine structure that changes the position of these lines -- an estimation of the energy range where the molecular lines of $^{87}Sr$ should be found.
\end{itemize}

In the following section I present the experimental protocol used on the $^{88}Sr$ species that enables to calibrate the procedure for fermionic molecular lines research.
\section{Spectroscopy protocol and calibration of the photoassociation laser}

\subsection{Gas preparation and isotope specificities}
I now present the experiments carried out to identify photoassociation lines of $^{87}\text{Sr}$,
starting with the molecular lines of $^{88}\text{Sr}$, which are well documented in the literature.
As explained in the introduction, the PA spectrum of $^{87}\text{Sr}$ has not been explored yet,
which is surprising since many molecular spectroscopy results have been published for the other
strontium isotopes.

The photoassociation (PA) rate strongly depends on the density, as detailed in Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling_laws}.
The experimental approach depends on the quantum statistics of the isotope:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fermions ($^{87}$Sr):} high densities are reached by optimizing the evaporative cooling ramps once the atoms are loaded into the crossed Optical Dipole Trap (ODT).
    \item \textbf{Bosons ($^{88}$Sr):} the near-zero scattering length of $^{88}$Sr prevents efficient evaporative cooling, as it suppresses the elastic collisions needed for thermalization. In this case, the
    final density depends almost only on the efficiency of the previous cooling and trapping stages (e.g. the MOT).
\end{itemize}

Once a dense cloud is obtained, the PA process is started in a magnetic field $B = 1.6$~G, chosen to maximize the $\pi$
optical transitions, by shining on the atoms a dedicated laser beam presented in detail below.

\begin{itemize}
    \item $f_{Red~master}$ is the optical frequency of the red master ECDL;
    \item $f^{RF}_{beating}$ is the frequency used to demodulate the beat note between the 11/2 beam and the MOGlabs laser. This RF source gives the error signal used to set
    and stabilize the frequency of the MOGlabs laser;
    \item $f^{RF}_{Raman}$ is the frequency of the "Raman AOM", which shifts the frequency of the PA beam at the very end of the optical path. This AOM is also used for the Raman transitions of the Ramsey interferometry
    schemes of Chapter~2;
    \item $f^{RF}_{slave 1}$ is the frequency of the "Slave 1" AOM, placed before the $^{87}\text{Sr}$ MOT beams and the "AOM 11/2" (used for optical pumping and for the beat note).
    Changing its diffraction order allows switching from $^{88}\text{Sr}$ to $^{87}\text{Sr}$ (see Sec.~\ref{sec:87Sr_inelastic});
    \item  $f^{RF}_{11/2}$ is the frequency of the "AOM 11/2", used for optical pumping and for the beat note.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

To address more deeply bound vibrational states, one can change the other frequencies (within their RF limits)
and/or invert the diffraction order of the "Raman AOM", which shifts the PA beam by about $\pm 260$~MHz.

\subsubsection{Spectral imperfections from the locking scheme}
The beating spectrum of the PA laser locking is presented in Fig.~\ref{beating
broadening} at a resolution bandwidth RBW = 10~kHz, with a video bandwidth VBW =
300~Hz. Since the expected molecular linewidth of $^{87}$Sr is $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi \simeq
15$~kHz, this signal gives the spectrum seen by the molecules. Fitting the beat
note with three Lorentzians -- a central peak and two symmetric sidebands -- gives a
central peak of FWHM $\simeq 13$~kHz, close to the RBW of the measurement, and
sidebands at $\pm 70$~kHz with a much larger FWHM of $\simeq 80$~kHz. Since the vertical
axis of the spectrum analyzer is a voltage, the optical power is proportional to its square:
integrating the squared signal, about two thirds of the optical power ($\simeq 65$--$67\%$)
addresses the molecules at the center frequency of the beat note, while about one third is
spread over the sidebands. This limits the power at which the atoms can be
photoassociated efficiently on resonance. Since the measured width of the central peak
is comparable to the RBW itself, this measurement cannot resolve the
intrinsic linewidth of the laser -- it only gives an upper bound. In any case, these
broad sidebands broaden the effective linewidth of the transition addressed
by the PA beam, whether atomic or molecular depending on the laser frequency, well beyond $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$.

\subsubsection{Spectral imperfections from the filter cavity system}
The MOGlabs laser passes through a Fabry-Perot cavity (see Sec.~\ref{FP cavity}) stabilized by a Pound-Drever-Hall (PDH) lock. In this scheme, an EOM modulates the phase of
the incident electric field, of frequency $f_0$, at $f_M=40.48$~MHz (see \cite{black_Gas_preparation_and_spin_control_2001}). For a modulation depth $\beta$, the phase modulation creates two sidebands around the carrier
(to first order):

\begin{equation}
    E_{inc}^{mod} = E_0 \left(J_0(\beta)e^{i2\pi f_0t}+J_1(\beta)e^{i2\pi(f_0 + f_M)t}-J_1(\beta)e^{i2\pi(f_0-f_M)t}\right)
\end{equation}

The first term, with the $0^{\text{th}}$-order Bessel function $J_0(\beta)$, is the carrier at frequency $f_0$, with an effective amplitude $E_0 J_0(\beta)$.
The second and third terms are two sidebands at $f_0\pm f_M$, with an amplitude given by the first-order Bessel function $J_1(\beta)$.
Figure~\ref{Bessel function} shows how the power is shared between the carrier and the sidebands as a function of $\beta$.
Experimentally, the modulation depth $\beta=1$ gives about three times more power in the carrier than in each sideband ($J_0^2(\beta)/J_1^2(\beta) \simeq 3.0$), which is a good compromise for the lock; $\beta$ is directly tuned by the amplitude
of the RF signal.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{Chapitres/Engineering highly entangled system of photoassociated 87Sr atoms/images/Figures_pour_theorie/bessel_functions_finale.pdf}
    \caption{Squared modulus of the $0^{\text{th}}$- and $1^{\text{st}}$-order Bessel functions $J_n$, which set the power distribution between the carrier and the PDH sidebands.}
    \label{Bessel function}
\end{figure}

The sidebands are essential to the PDH scheme itself: their reflection on the cavity, interfering with the reflected carrier, produces the dispersive error signal used for the lock (see Sec.~\ref{FP cavity}). Since they are detuned by $f_M$ from the carrier resonance, they are in principle mostly reflected by the cavity rather than transmitted to the atoms. However, this rejection is not perfect, since the cavity linewidth is finite: the sidebands still have a small residual transmission set by the Lorentzian lineshape of the cavity,
\begin{equation}
\frac{T(f_M)}{T(0)} = \frac{1}{1+\left(\dfrac{2f_M}{\delta\nu}\right)^2}.
\end{equation}
Using the cavity's measured finesse ($\mathcal{F} = 310 \pm 15$) and FSR ($3~\text{GHz}$), i.e. a linewidth $\delta\nu = \text{FSR}/\mathcal{F} \simeq 10~\text{MHz}$, and the PDH modulation frequency $f_M = 40.48~\text{MHz}$, this residual transmission amounts to $T(f_M)/T(0) \simeq 1.4\%$. Combined with the sideband-to-carrier power ratio set by the modulation depth ($J_1^2(\beta)/J_0^2(\beta) \simeq 0.33$ at $\beta=1$), the power reaching the atoms in each sideband is expected to be $\simeq 0.5\%$ of the power reaching them in the carrier. Although small, this residual power is enough to drive a detectable one-body loss signal, displaced from the main photoassociation resonance by $\pm f_M$, as we now demonstrate experimentally.

We measured the effect of these sidebands on a gas of $500~000$ atoms, evaporated and recompressed to a temperature $T = 4.1~\mu$K, in the two spin components $m_F=-9/2$ and $+9/2$
prepared by optical pumping. The gas was illuminated for $2$~s at an intensity $I = 385$~mW/cm$^2$ with the MOGlabs ECDL.
As sketched in Fig.~\ref{sidebands}, the sidebands produce "ghost" resonances when the carrier is detuned by $\pm f_M = \pm 40.48$~MHz
from the atomic transition ($f_0' = f_0 \pm f_M$), leading to parasitic one-body losses.
In the purple case of Fig.~\ref{sidebands}, the lower sideband at $f_0'-f_M$ addresses the atomic transition at $f_0$, with a smaller amplitude than the carrier
(black) when the carrier itself is on resonance.

This resonance is not a single, clean line but shows several overlapping peaks, which we attribute to two possible causes. First, the PA laser polarization may not be perfectly $\pi$: a small $\sigma^\pm$ component would drive transitions to neighboring $m_F$ states,
each addressed at a slightly different frequency and all overlapping within the scan range. Second, several lines shifted by the Zeeman effect and by the tensor light shift (TLS) may contribute at the same time; this is supported by the same measurement repeated at different magnetic fields, where the splitting between the peaks changed with $B$. These two explanations can both be true, and separating their contributions would require dedicated measurements.

Lowering the modulation depth $\beta$ reduced the amplitude of this signal, but did not remove it completely: a weak molecular line could still be hidden in this signal. The other laser (injected from 'AOM 11/2') could be used to explore this frequency range.

\subsection*{Conclusion on the characterization of the PA spectroscopic protocol}

This section described how the PA laser frequency is set and checked. The characterization of the laser system
gave two important results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spectral distribution of the power:} the beat note shows that about one third
    of the laser power is in unwanted frequency components. This contributes to the observed broadening of both
    atomic and molecular lines.

    \item \textbf{Parasitic resonances from the modulation:} a modulation of the laser creates sidebands that can drive unexpected atomic or molecular transitions.
    Here, the sidebands at $\pm f_M = \pm 40.48$~MHz from the carrier can lead to a misinterpretation of the atom loss signals. In our setup, this modulation
    comes from the electro-optic modulator (EOM) of the Pound-Drever-Hall (PDH) lock, but similar effects can appear with any optical element
    driven by an RF source, such as acousto-optic modulators (AOMs).
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Atomic and molecular lines calibration}
\subsubsection{General experimental framework and baseline parameters}
\label{sec:88Sr_framework}

Before scanning for molecular resonances, the PA laser frequency is referenced to the atomic
transition, by recording the atom loss as a function of the detuning around the atomic
resonance (Fig.~\ref{88Sr resonance}, top). This loss is a one-body process: the atoms scatter
photons and are heated out of the trap.

The molecular lines, in contrast, induce two-body losses, $\dot n=-K_2n^2$, so that
$1/n(t)-1/n_0=K_2t$, with a characteristic loss time, after which the density is half the initial density,
\begin{equation}
\tau = \frac{1}{K_2 n_0}.
\label{temps caractéristique}
\end{equation}

Once the MOGlabs laser is locked to this reference, $f^{RF}_{beating}$ is
scanned to search for the least-bound vibrational states; the two lines studied here are shown in
Fig.~\ref{88Sr resonance}.

\subsubsection{Fitting model for molecular resonances}
\label{sec:fitting_model}

Each data point of the presented atomic and molecular lines is extracted by fitting the
absorption images of the atomic cloud with a 2D Gaussian function of amplitude $A$ and widths $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$.
The atom number $N$
is obtained from the integral of the fit, $N \propto A\,\sigma_x\,\sigma_y$. The relative
statistical uncertainty on $N$ was estimated from the diagonal elements of the fit
covariance matrix,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\delta N}{N} \simeq \sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta A}{A}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta \sigma_x}{\sigma_x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta \sigma_y}{\sigma_y}\right)^2},
\end{equation}
where $\delta A$, $\delta \sigma_x$, and $\delta \sigma_y$ are the standard errors on the
amplitude and on the spatial widths along $x$ and $y$. This expression neglects the
off-diagonal covariance terms between the fit parameters (e.g.
$\text{Cov}(A,\sigma_x)$), which were not retained from the original fitting routine. In a
Gaussian fit, the amplitude and the widths are anticorrelated, since the derivatives of
the model with respect to $A$ and to $\sigma_{x,y}$ have the same sign over the whole image:
a slightly broader profile with a slightly lower amplitude yields the same integral. These
neglected terms are therefore negative, and the expression above only gives a slightly
larger, i.e. conservative, uncertainty on $N$; a simulation of our fit gives a factor
$\simeq1.6$ between the two estimates. This has no practical consequence, since this fit
uncertainty ($\simeq0.3$--$0.5\%$) is negligible compared to the shot-to-shot fluctuation of the
atom number included below.

To account for shot-to-shot experimental fluctuations, the relative RMS fluctuation of the
atom number, $\sigma_{\text{shot}}$, measured over ten consecutive realizations, is added in
quadrature to the relative fit uncertainty $\delta N/N$ defined above. The total uncertainty
on each atom number is thus
\begin{equation}
\sigma_N^{\text{tot}} = N \sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta N}{N}\right)^2 + \sigma_{\text{shot}}^2}.
\end{equation}

The temperature of the cloud is obtained from the same absorption images, by time of flight
(TOF), assuming an isotropic thermal velocity distribution. During the TOF, the cloud expands
ballistically, and its Gaussian width $\sigma_y$ along the vertical axis evolves as
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_y^2 = \sigma_{0,y}^2 + v_{\text{th}}^2 \, \text{TOF}^2,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{0,y}$ is the \textit{in-situ} RMS width of the gas in the optical dipole trap
(ODT) and $v_{\text{th}}$ is the thermal velocity. For a Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity
distribution, the equipartition theorem gives $\frac{1}{2} m_{\text{at}} v_{\text{th}}^2 = \frac{1}{2} k_B T$
along each direction, so that the temperature is obtained from the width $\sigma_y$ measured on the image:
\begin{equation}
    T = \frac{m_{\text{at}} \left(\sigma_y^2 - \sigma_{0,y}^2\right)}{k_B \, \text{TOF}^2}.
\end{equation}
Assuming independent uncertainties on the in-situ size $\sigma_{0,y}$ and on the expanded size
$\sigma_y$, error propagation gives
\begin{equation}
    \delta T = \frac{2 m_{\text{at}}}{k_B \, \text{TOF}^2} \sqrt{\left(\sigma_y \delta \sigma_y\right)^2 + \left(\sigma_{0,y} \delta \sigma_{0,y}\right)^2}.
\label{eq:deltaT}
\end{equation}

The atom numbers measured at different detunings form a spectrum, which is then fitted.
Each atomic and molecular resonance is fitted with a Lorentzian lineshape,
\begin{equation}
    f(x) = y_0 + \frac{A}{1 + \left(\frac{x-x_0}{\Gamma/2}\right)^2},
    \label{eq:lorentzian_generic}
\end{equation}
centered at $x_0$, with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) $\Gamma$, a peak amplitude $A$
(negative for a loss) and an offset $y_0$. For the normalized spectra of $^{87}\text{Sr}$
(Sec.~\ref{raie sr87 }), the \textit{depth} of a line is the peak amplitude divided by the offset,
$|A|/y_0$.

The resonances exhibit an effective linewidth $\Gamma_{\text{tot}}$ resulting from
several physical and technical broadening mechanisms. Some of them can be
estimated from first principles, while others -- such as the spectral impurities
of the laser, an inhomogeneous light shift across the cloud, or additional decay
channels of the molecular state like predissociation -- cannot be given a simple
expression and are only accessible through the measured width. We therefore do
not attempt to model $\Gamma_{\text{tot}}$: it is measured on each spectrum and used
as such in the determination of $l_{\text{opt}}$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:determining_lopt}).
The known contributions are nevertheless useful as orders of magnitude.

The first contribution, $\Gamma_0 \equiv \Gamma_{\text{rad}}$, is associated with the radiative lifetime of the
excited state. For a photoassociated molecule -- formed through a one-photon process --
this natural radiative linewidth is, in principle, twice that of an excited atom
($\Gamma_{\text{at}} = 2\pi \times 7.5~\text{kHz}$ for the $^1S_0 \rightarrow {}^3P_1$ atomic line of
$^{88}\text{Sr}$, and $\Gamma_0 \approx 2\pi\times15~\text{kHz}$ on average for the molecular
states). Due to small Franck-Condon factors, the effective saturation intensity
$I_{\text{sat}}^{\text{eff}}$ for molecular transitions is significantly larger than for
atomic ones, making power broadening completely negligible for molecular lines
\cite{zelevinsky_narrow_2006}.

For molecular resonances, the Doppler shift has to be calculated for an atom pair rather
than for a single atom. The thermal broadening is thus of order
\begin{equation}
\Gamma_{\text{thermal}} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{k_B T}{2m_{\text{at}}}}
\end{equation}
with $2m_{\text{at}}$ the mass of the pair, $\lambda$ the laser wavelength, $T$ the gas
temperature, and $k_B$ the Boltzmann constant.

Finally, the spectral width of the laser source and any additional technical or
physical broadening (light shift inhomogeneity, predissociation, unresolved
structure) add to these two contributions. The light shift inhomogeneity comes from the
light shift of the molecular level, which is proportional to the local intensity: since
the intensity of the Gaussian PA beam, and of the trapping beams when they are on at the
same time, varies across the cloud, pairs at different positions -- in particular the
hotter atoms exploring the edges of the trap -- see the line at slightly different
frequencies, which broadens the measured line (for the $\nu=-1$ state of
$^{88}\text{Sr}$, the light shift reaches $\simeq200$~kHz for $0.8$~W/cm$^2$
\cite{blatt_measurement_2011}, see Sec.~\ref{sec:88Sr_results}). Since these mechanisms produce
different line shapes (Lorentzian widths add linearly, Gaussian widths in
quadrature), the difference between $\Gamma_{\text{tot}}$ and the known
contributions $\Gamma_0/2\pi$ and $\Gamma_{\text{thermal}}$ only gives an order of
magnitude of the excess broadening, and is not decomposed further. It is common
for photoassociation lines to appear broader than expected from the radiative and
thermal widths alone.


\subsubsection{Decay dynamics ($dN/dt$)}
\label{decay dynamics}
The usual test of the inelastic nature of the collisions is the time evolution of the atom number, $N(t)$, discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling_laws}. This curve allows one to (i) check the presence of two-body losses and (ii)
determine the loss coefficient $K_2$, hence $l_{\text{opt}}/I$. The fitting function is obtained by integrating the rate equation:

\begin{gather}
    \frac{dN(t,T)}{dt} = -\beta(T(t)) N(t,T)^2 - \Gamma N(t,T) \label{eq globale collisions} \\[1.5ex]
    \text{Setting } M(t,T) = N(t,T)e^{\Gamma t} \iff N(t,T) = M(t,T)e^{-\Gamma t} \label{eq:change_var} \\[1.5ex]
    \dot{M}(t,T)e^{-\Gamma t} - \Gamma M(t,T) e^{-\Gamma t} = -\beta(T(t)) M(t,T)^2 e^{-2\Gamma t} - \Gamma M(t,T) e^{-\Gamma t} \label{eq:diff} \\[1.5ex]
    \int_{0}^{t} \frac{\dot{M}(t',T)}{M(t',T)^2} \, dt' = -\int_{0}^{t} \beta(T(t')) e^{-\Gamma t'} \, dt' \label{eq:integ} \\[1.5ex]
    -\frac{1}{M(t,T)} + \frac{1}{N_0} = -\int_{0}^{t} \beta(T(t')) e^{-\Gamma t'} \, dt' \label{eq:inv_M} \\[1.5ex]
    M(t,T) = \frac{N_0}{1 + N_0 \int_{0}^{t} \beta(T(t')) e^{-\Gamma t'} \, dt'} \label{eq:sol_M} \\[1.5ex]
    N(t,T) = \frac{N_0 e^{-\Gamma t}}{1 + N_0 \int_{0}^{t} \beta(T(t')) e^{-\Gamma t'} \, dt'} \label{eq:final_fit}
\end{gather}

Since $N$ depends on the temperature, Eq.~\ref{eq:final_fit} is rewritten to show explicitly the $T$-dependence of $\beta(T)$. The atoms are trapped in an ODT with a mean trap frequency
$\bar{\omega} = (\omega_x \omega_y \omega_z)^{1/3}$. According to the equipartition theorem, the width $\sigma_{i}$ (with $i=\{x,y,z\}$) of the Gaussian distribution is related to the temperature $T$ by
$\frac{1}{2}m_{\text{at}}\omega_{i}^2\sigma_{i}^2 = \frac{1}{2}k_B T$. This gives the effective volume

\begin{equation}
    V_e(T) = (2\pi)^{3/2}\left(\frac{k_B T}{m_{\text{at}}}\right)^{3/2} \frac{1}{\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z} = (2\pi)^{3/2}\left(\frac{k_B T}{m_{\text{at}}\bar{\omega}^2}\right)^{3/2}
\end{equation}

where $\bar{\omega}$ depends on the final power of the ODT beams. Substituting $\beta(T(t')) = \frac{K_2}{2\sqrt{2}V_e(T(t'))}$ into Eq.~\ref{eq:final_fit}, the fitting function becomes

\begin{equation}
    N(t,T) = \frac{N_0 e^{-\Gamma t}}{1 + \left[ \frac{K_2}{8\pi^{3/2}} \left( m_{\text{at}} \right)^{3/2} \omega_x\omega_y\omega_z \right] N_0 \int_{0}^{t} \left( \frac{1}{k_B T(t')} \right)^{3/2} e^{-\Gamma t'} \, dt'}
\label{equation fit finale}
\end{equation}

Since the gas heats up during photoassociation, $K_2$ is extracted from a measured decay by letting $\beta$ depend on the
measured temperature. The temperature measurements are first linearly interpolated into a continuous function $T(t)$; this
interpolated temperature is fixed once and for all, independently of the fit that
follows. The nonlinear fit then adjusts four free parameters
-- the one-body loss rate $\Gamma$, the two-body loss coefficient $K_2$
(equivalently $l_{\text{opt}}$), the initial atom number $N_0$, and a constant
offset added to Eq.~\ref{equation fit finale}. At each iteration, the current value of $K_2$ is combined with the
interpolated $T(t)$ to compute the instantaneous loss rate
$\beta(t) = K_2/(2\sqrt{2}\,V_e(T(t)))$ at every time step; $\beta(t)$ is then
integrated numerically to compute a trial decay curve $N(t)$ with
Eq.~\ref{equation fit finale}. This trial curve is compared to the measured $N(t)$, and
the four parameters are updated to reduce the $\chi^2$, until convergence.
The best-fit $K_2$ is thus a single constant for the whole curve, while $\beta(t)$ changes during the
decay only because $T(t)$ changes -- not because $K_2$ itself changes with time. When the temperature
changes little during the decay, $\beta$ is simply taken constant.

From Eq.~\ref{eq globale collisions}, the decay is governed by two dynamics with different
characteristic times: the two-body time $1/(\beta N_0)$, which sets the initial decay when the
density is high, and the one-body time $1/\Gamma$. As the atom number decreases, the two-body
loss rate $\beta N(t)$ decreases, and one-body losses take over when $\beta N(t)\simeq\Gamma$,
i.e. when $N\simeq\Gamma/\beta$. There is no sharp break between these two dynamics but a
smooth crossover: on a logarithmic scale, the one-body dynamics appears as a straight line,
whereas the two-body dynamics appears as a faster, non-exponential decay whose rate
continuously decreases.

\subsubsection{Determining the optical scattering length $l_{\text{opt}}$}
\label{sec:determining_lopt}

The optical length $l_{\text{opt}}$ characterizes the strength of a PA line
independently of the experimental conditions: it scales linearly with the laser
intensity and depends only on the Franck--Condon overlap between the scattering
state and the molecular state (Eq.~\ref{lopt_def}). It is however never measured
directly. Two observables are available for each line: (i) the loss spectrum,
i.e. the atom number after a fixed illumination time as a function of the laser
detuning, which gives the line center and its total width; (ii) the decay $N(t)$
of the atom number at fixed detuning, which gives the two-body loss coefficient
$K_2$ at that detuning. I first recall how $l_{\text{opt}}$ is defined from the
loss coefficient of an ideal, unbroadened line, then show how this definition
must be adapted to the broadened lines actually observed, and finally describe
the two methods used to obtain the required loss coefficient.

\textbf{Ideal line: definition of $l_{\text{opt}}$.}

Following the formulation of \cite{zelevinsky_narrow_2006}, written as in \cite{blatt_systematic_2011} to allow for
extra molecular decay ($\Gamma_{\text{mol}}\geq\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$), the microscopic
two-body loss rate coefficient near a photoassociation resonance is given by:
\begin{equation}
    K_2(\Delta) = \frac{4\pi\hbar}{\mu} \frac{\Gamma_{\text{mol}}\Gamma_{\text{rad}} \, l_{\text{opt}}}{\left(\frac{E}{\hbar} + 2\pi\Delta\right)^2 + \frac{(\Gamma_{\text{stim}} + \Gamma_{\text{mol}})^2}{4}}
\label{eq:zelevinsky_K2}
\end{equation}
where $\mu = m_{\text{at}}/2$ is the reduced mass of the atom pair,
$\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ is the total molecular linewidth and $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ its radiative part (both in rad/s),
$\Gamma_{\text{stim}}$ is the light-induced stimulated width (in rad/s), $\Delta$
is the laser detuning expressed as a frequency (in Hz), so that $2\pi\Delta$ is the angular
detuning used in Eq.~\ref{K2 complet Blatt}, and $E$ is the relative collision energy.

Experimentally, we measure the relative atomic loss profile
$f(\Delta) = \frac{n_0 - n(t)}{n_0}$ as a function of the laser detuning $\Delta$.
Considering only the two-body loss term, $n(t) = \frac{n_0}{1+K_2(\Delta)\,n_0 t_{\text{PA}}}$
(see Eq.~\ref{temps caractéristique}), so that for a pulse duration $t_{\text{PA}}$
and an average atomic density $\langle n \rangle$, in the limit of small loss
fraction ($K_2 \langle n \rangle t_{\text{PA}} \ll 1$), the relative loss is
directly proportional to $K_2(\Delta)$:
\begin{equation}
    f(\Delta) \approx \langle n \rangle \, t_{\text{PA}} \, K_2(\Delta)
\end{equation}
i.e. the initial loss rate $f(\Delta)/t_{\text{PA}} \simeq \langle n\rangle K_2(\Delta)$ directly gives the
two-body loss coefficient.
Fitting this measured resonance profile with a Lorentzian function yields:
\begin{equation}
    f(\Delta) = \frac{A}{1 + \left( \frac{\Delta + E/h}{\Gamma_{\text{fit}}/2} \right)^2}
\label{eq:lorentzian_fit_standard}
\end{equation}
where $A$ is the dimensionless amplitude of the loss peak and $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$
is the full width at half maximum (FWHM), both $\Delta$ and $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$ being in Hz.
Factoring $(2\pi)^2$ out of the denominator of Eq.~\ref{eq:zelevinsky_K2} shows that it is
indeed a Lorentzian in $\Delta$, centered at $-E/h$ and of FWHM
$(\Gamma_{\text{stim}}+\Gamma_{\text{mol}})/2\pi$.

If the molecular line had no other width than its radiative one ($\Gamma_{\text{mol}}=\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$), the fit parameters would map
directly onto Eq.~\ref{eq:zelevinsky_K2}, with
$\Gamma_{\text{fit}} = (\Gamma_{\text{stim}} + \Gamma_{\text{mol}})/2\pi \simeq \Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi$
in the threshold regime $\Gamma_{\text{stim}} \ll \Gamma_{\text{mol}}$. The loss
coefficient at the line center would then reach
\begin{equation}
    K_2(0) = \frac{A}{\langle n\rangle t_{\text{PA}}} = \frac{16h}{m_{\text{at}}}\,l_{\text{opt}},
    \label{eq:K2_peak_ideal}
\end{equation}
which is Eq.~\ref{K2 et lopt} for $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}=\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$. This is how $l_{\text{opt}}$ is defined: it is
proportional to the peak loss coefficient of the \textit{ideal} line, whose width is
the radiative width $\Gamma_{\text{rad}} \simeq 2\pi\times15$~kHz. In our
experiment, however, the measured widths $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$ exceed
$\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi$ by a factor $\simeq20$ for $^{88}\text{Sr}$
(Table~\ref{tab:88Sr-lines-summary}) and $10^2$--$10^3$ for $^{87}\text{Sr}$
(Table~\ref{tab:summary_fits_PA_final}). Applying Eq.~\ref{eq:K2_peak_ideal} to the
measured peak loss coefficient would then strongly underestimate $l_{\text{opt}}$,
as shown below.

\textbf{Broadened line: integration over the full linewidth.}

The observed lines are much broader than $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$, because of the laser
frequency noise, the thermal distribution of the atoms, an inhomogeneous light shift
or additional decay channels of the molecular state. The
decay recorded with the laser set at the line center only collects the losses that
occur within a width of order $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi\simeq15$~kHz around the
center, i.e. a fraction $(\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi)/\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$ of the line.
The value of $K_2$ measured at the center, $K_2^{\text{fit}}(0)$, is therefore much
lower than the ideal peak value $\frac{16h}{m_{\text{at}}}l_{\text{opt}}$ of
Eq.~\ref{eq:K2_peak_ideal}. To characterize the line, the losses must be considered
over its whole fitted width $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$.

The broadening does not create or remove losses: it only spreads them over a broader
frequency range. The area under the line, i.e. its height times its width (the factor
$\pi/2$ of a Lorentzian being the same for both), is thus the same for the ideal line
and for the observed one:
\begin{equation}
    K_2^{\text{fit}}(0)\times\Gamma_{\text{fit}}
    = \frac{16h}{m_{\text{at}}}\,l_{\text{opt}}\times\frac{\Gamma_{\text{rad}}}{2\pi}
    \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
    l_{\text{opt}} = \frac{m_{\text{at}}}{16h}\;K_2^{\text{fit}}(0)\;
    \frac{\Gamma_{\text{fit}}}{\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi}.
    \label{eq:lopt_corrected}
\end{equation}
This relation does not require $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}=\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$: from Eq.~\ref{K2 complet Blatt}, an extra
molecular decay lowers the peak by $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ and widens the line by
$\Gamma_{\text{mol}}/\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$, so that the area, $\frac{16h}{m_{\text{at}}}l_{\text{opt}}\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi$,
is independent of $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$: $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$ includes this extra decay as well as the other
broadening mechanisms.
In other words, the optical length obtained from the ideal-line relation must be
multiplied by the ratio of the fitted width to the radiative width. This ratio is of
order $20$ for the $^{88}\text{Sr}$ lines and $10^2$--$10^3$ for the
$^{87}\text{Sr}$ lines.

This is equivalent to the empirical broadening factor $\eta\geq1$ used in Ref.~\cite{reschovsky_narrow-line_2018}, where the line remains Lorentzian with a width $\eta\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ and a peak loss coefficient lowered by $\eta$ at fixed $l_{\text{opt}}$, here with $\eta=\Gamma_{\text{fit}}/(\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi)$.

Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected} requires two inputs: the width $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$ and the
loss coefficient at the line center $K_2^{\text{fit}}(0)$. The width can only be obtained
from a spectrum, i.e. from the Lorentzian fit of the loss spectrum, and is common to both
methods below. The two methods differ in the way the peak value $K_2^{\text{fit}}(0)$ is
measured: from the depth of the same spectrum (Method 1), or from an independent
measurement of the atom number decay with the laser set at the line center (Method 2).
Comparing the two methods is therefore a test of the consistency between the spectrum
and the decay measurement.

\textbf{Method 1: Lorentzian fit of the loss spectrum.}

The Lorentzian fit of the loss spectrum (Eq.~\ref{eq:lorentzian_fit_standard}) gives
both the amplitude $A$ and the width $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$ of the line. In the limit of
small losses, the amplitude gives the loss coefficient at the line center,
\begin{equation}
    K_2^{\text{fit}}(0) = \frac{A}{\langle n\rangle\,t_{\text{PA}}},
    \label{eq:K2_from_spectrum}
\end{equation}
and inserting it into Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected} yields
\begin{equation}
    l_{\text{opt}} = \frac{m_{\text{at}}}{16h}\,
    \frac{A\,\Gamma_{\text{fit}}}{\langle n\rangle\,t_{\text{PA}}\,\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi}.
\end{equation}
Compared with the ideal-line mapping, in which $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$ would be the
molecular width itself and $l_{\text{opt}}$ would scale as $A\,\Gamma_{\text{fit}}^2$,
only one power of $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}$ remains: the width here reflects the
broadening of the line, not the lifetime of the molecular state. For the
losses of up to $40\%$ observed on $^{87}\text{Sr}$, the small-loss limit is not accurate. As
in Refs.~\cite{zelevinsky_narrow_2006,blatt_systematic_2011}, where the loss spectra
are fitted with the solution of the two-body rate equation rather than with its
linearized form, we use the exact relation given by the derivation of the
characteristic time (Eq.~\ref{temps caractéristique}): $1/n(t)-1/n_0=K_2\,t$, i.e.
$K_2\,n_0\,t=n_0/n(t)-1=A/(1-A)$ at the line center, where $A=(n_0-n)/n_0$. In
Eq.~\ref{eq:K2_from_spectrum}, $A$ is therefore replaced by $A/(1-A)$, the two
expressions being equal for small losses. As in these references, the temperature is
assumed constant during the pulse, so that the heating of the gas is neglected. Both
inputs come from the same spectrum, at the same intensity and density.

\textbf{Method 2: atom number decay at the line center.}

The laser is set at the line center and the decay $N(t)$ is fitted with
Eq.~\ref{eq:final_fit}, the temperature being interpolated from the measured
$T(t)$ (Sec.~\ref{decay dynamics}). This yields $K_2^{\text{fit}}(0)$ while
accounting for heating, but relies on a width measured on a separate spectrum.
If the two measurements are not performed at the same intensity, a light shift can
displace the line so that the decay is no longer recorded at its center, and an
intensity-dependent broadening makes the width measured at lower intensity too
small: both effects lead to an underestimation of $l_{\text{opt}}$. The fit may
also attribute to $K_2$ a curvature of the decay that is not due to
photoassociation (see Fig.~\ref{exp decay 6 MHz noise}), which would lead to an
overestimation.

Since both methods measure the same quantity $K_2^{\text{fit}}(0)$, their
comparison under identical conditions is a direct consistency test. In the
following, $l_{\text{opt}}$ is given for both methods through
Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected}, and their difference is taken as a systematic
uncertainty.

\subsubsection{Estimation of the uncertainties}
\label{sec:uncertainties}
The quantities used in the following to characterize the PA lines are given with
uncertainties estimated as follows; they are combined in quadrature.

\textit{Temperature.} The temperature is measured by time of flight from the vertical
size $\sigma_y$ of the cloud (Sec.~\ref{sec:fitting_model}). Two uncertainties are used:
\begin{itemize}
    \item for a single temperature point, the error bar comes from the uncertainty
    $\delta\sigma_y$ of the Gaussian fit of the image (Eq.~\ref{eq:deltaT});
    \item for the temperature of a whole spectrum, we give the mean temperature of all the
    points of the spectrum, and their standard deviation as uncertainty. This standard
    deviation, which reflects the shot-to-shot fluctuations, is much larger than the fit
    uncertainty of a single point.
\end{itemize}
For the decays, we give the temperatures of the first and last points, with their fit
uncertainty. The same relative uncertainty is taken for the $^{88}\text{Sr}$ data. It propagates to the thermal width $\Gamma_{\text{thermal}}\propto\sqrt{T}$ as
\begin{equation}
\frac{\delta\Gamma_{\text{thermal}}}{\Gamma_{\text{thermal}}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\delta T}{T}.
\end{equation}

\textit{PA intensity.} The PA intensity is computed as $I=2P/(\pi w_xw_y)$, so that
\begin{equation}
\frac{\delta I}{I}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta P}{P}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\delta w_x}{w_x}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\delta w_y}{w_y}\right)^2}.
\end{equation}
The uncertainty $\delta w$ on a waist measured with our camera is estimated from the scatter
of repeated measurements around the fitted beam caustic (z-scan of the Reservoir beam), and
$\delta P$ is the resolution of the power meter. For the $^{88}\text{Sr}$ data, only the waist
uncertainty is included.

\textit{Mean trap frequency and density.} The mean trap frequency
$\bar\omega=(\omega_x\omega_y\omega_z)^{1/3}$ is computed for each measurement from the measured
Reservoir and Dimple beam powers -- at the end of the evaporation ramps -- and the trap geometry, and gives the average density
$\langle n\rangle=N/(2\sqrt{2}\,V_e(T))$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:3.27}). This estimate has not been
cross-checked against a direct trap-frequency measurement (e.g. parametric heating).
The uncertainty $\delta\bar\omega$ is obtained by varying, in the trap model, the waists of the
Reservoir and Dimple beams by their uncertainty and their powers by the resolution of the
power meter. Since $V_e\propto\bar\omega^{-3}$, it propagates to the density as
\begin{equation}
\frac{\delta\langle n\rangle}{\langle n\rangle}=3\,\frac{\delta\bar\omega}{\bar\omega}.
\end{equation}

\textit{Loss coefficient and optical length.} The uncertainty on $K_2$ combines the
statistical uncertainty $\delta K_2^{\text{fit}}$ of the fit (depth of the loss spectrum, or
two-body coefficient of the decay) with that of the density,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\delta K_2}{K_2}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta K_2^{\text{fit}}}{K_2}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\delta\langle n\rangle}{\langle n\rangle}\right)^2},
\end{equation}
and the uncertainty on $l_{\text{opt}}/I$ additionally includes those of the intensity and,
when the linewidth is accounted for, of the fitted width:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\delta(l_{\text{opt}}/I)}{l_{\text{opt}}/I}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta K_2}{K_2}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\delta I}{I}\right)^2+\left(\frac{\delta\Gamma_{\text{fit}}}{\Gamma_{\text{fit}}}\right)^2}.
\end{equation}

Both states were detected using the MOGlabs laser system. The natural width $\Gamma_0$
and the thermal broadening $\Gamma_{\text{thermal}}$ are both estimated from first
principles rather than extracted from the data -- the expected linewidth should in
principle also include a power broadening contribution, which we take to be negligible
here given the small Franck-Condon factors discussed above. The measured widths
exceed $\Gamma_0/2\pi + \Gamma_{\text{thermal}} \simeq 27$~kHz by far: the excess broadening,
of order $300$~kHz, captures both technical laser broadening and any deviation
of these first-principles estimates from their true values.
A few points lie far from the Lorentzian fits of the molecular lines
(Fig.~\ref{88Sr resonance}, middle and bottom). The corresponding absorption images were
checked and show a normal cloud, correctly described by the 2D Gaussian fit: these
deviations do not come from the image analysis but from uncontrolled shot-to-shot
fluctuations of the experiment, and the points are kept in the data.

With the optical lengths measured by Blatt et al.~\cite{blatt_systematic_2011} for these two
lines, our densities and intensities, and once the measured linewidths are accounted for
(Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected}), one expects a characteristic time $\tau$
(Eq.~\ref{temps caractéristique}) of a few ms for both lines. This is what we observe on the
spectrum of the $\nu=-2$ line, whereas the losses on the spectrum of the $\nu=-1$ line are
about two orders of magnitude slower. We attribute this to a misalignment of the PA beam with
respect to the atomic cloud during these measurements, visible as a distorted beam profile at
the output of the vacuum cell: only part of the cloud sees the nominal intensity, and this
fraction can change between measurements. The effective intensity is therefore poorly known for
these data, which limits the comparison with the literature to the line positions and to orders
of magnitude for $l_{\text{opt}}$. This misalignment only concerns the $^{88}\text{Sr}$
measurements shown in this section, not the $^{87}\text{Sr}$ data.

Our measured line centers can be compared to the zero-intensity line centers determined by
Blatt et al.~\cite{blatt_measurement_2011}, who extrapolated the light shift induced by the PA
beam to zero intensity. 

\paragraph{$\nu =-1$ state}
For the $\nu=-1$ state (their $n=-2$), we measure $-23.99(1)$~MHz,
in agreement with their $-23.98(1)$~MHz (and close to the $-23.932(33)$~MHz of Zelevinsky
et al.~\cite{zelevinsky_narrow_2006}). The light shift of this state, $(-251\pm67)$~kHz per
W/cm$^2$ of PA intensity \cite{blatt_measurement_2011}, i.e. a red shift of $\simeq200$~kHz
for $0.8$~W/cm$^2$, amounts to only $\simeq-2$~kHz at the $8$~mW/cm$^2$ used here. 


\paragraph{$\nu =-2$ state}
For the $\nu=-2$ state (their $n=-3$), we measure $-222.70(1)$~MHz, about $0.5$~MHz further from the
asymptote than their $-222.18(5)$~MHz and the $-222.161(35)$~MHz of Zelevinsky et al. This
difference is not explained by the light shift of this state, $(29\pm2)$~kHz per W/cm$^2$,
i.e. only $\simeq+24$~kHz at the $826$~mW/cm$^2$ used here, and towards the blue. Blatt et al.
also report a light shift induced by the trapping light, which broadens the lines on their
blue side \cite{blatt_systematic_2011}. 

The measured linewidths ($\Gamma_{\text{tot}} \simeq 330\text{--}344$~kHz) remain
significantly larger than the expected intrinsic molecular linewidth of
$\Gamma_0/2\pi \approx 15$~kHz. Part of this broadening could come from the spectral
impurities of the MOGlabs laser. However, convolving the power spectrum of the beat note
(Fig.~\ref{beating broadening}) -- a narrow central peak (FWHM $\simeq 13$~kHz) with
sidebands at $\pm 70$~kHz of FWHM $\simeq 80$~kHz -- with the expected clean molecular lineshape
($\Gamma_0/2\pi \oplus \Gamma_{\text{thermal}} \simeq 19$~kHz) only gives an apparent linewidth of
$\simeq 30$~kHz. The structure of this beat note therefore explains only a small part of the
observed broadening: other technical noise sources, not captured by this beat-note
measurement, must contribute. To
confirm the technical origin of this broadening, we measured the same molecular line
with an alternative, higher-stability laser source.

\subsubsection{Noise of the diode}
\label{sec:diode_noise}

When comparing the two lasers, extra loss peaks appeared with the injection-locked source,
at $\pm 6$~MHz and $\pm 12$~MHz from $f_0$. Their symmetric position, on both the red and the
blue side, points to sidebands, which come from noise on the diode.

At $\delta = -6$~MHz, on the red side of the resonance (Fig.~\ref{6MHz noise red detuned}), no structure is resolved: the atom
number only shows noise, with large shot-to-shot fluctuations caused by the heating of the
SU(10) gas at $T=1.6~\mu$K (with a fit uncertainty of $\simeq0.1~\mu$K, see
Sec.~\ref{sec:uncertainties}).

The same scan, taken on the blue side (Fig.~\ref{6MHz noise blue detuned}), clearly resolves
the $m_F$ projections: the twelve Zeeman sublevels $-11/2 \leq m_F \leq 11/2$ of the
$^3P_1, F=11/2$ excited state appear around $+6$~MHz and $+12$~MHz. This confirms that the
peaks are atomic, not molecular. Each of these resonances removes up to $\simeq50\%$ of the
atoms, although in the SU(10) gas a single Zeeman-resolved transition only addresses one of
the ten ground-state spin components, i.e. $\simeq10\%$ of the atoms. The atoms that scatter
photons are heated by the recoil and share this energy with all the other spin components
through elastic collisions; the whole gas then heats up and atoms of all spin states are lost
by evaporation from the finite-depth trap. A small resonant fraction can thus deplete a large
part of the cloud.
None of these features -- carrier, red sideband, blue sideband -- is a single line:
each covers a rather wide range of detunings. This width comes from many single-atom
transitions, one for each $m_F$ sublevel, broadened by heating, rather than from
the laser noise itself. The whole pattern is simply displaced in frequency, appearing at
$\pm 6$~MHz and $\pm 12$~MHz as a consequence of a sideband in the laser spectrum.
As a result, searching for molecular lines
near $\pm 6$~MHz and $\pm 12$~MHz with the $F=11/2$ beam is compromised: a weak molecular
signal there would be hidden within this atomic structure.

The loss coefficient obtained from these decays is the loss coefficient at the

the value obtained by applying directly the ideal-line relation

the two-body coefficient $\beta$ fitted on the decay (with $\dot N=-\beta N^2-\Gamma N$ and a constant
temperature): $K_2=2\sqrt{2}\,V_e\,\beta$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:3.28}), with $V_e$ computed from the mean trap
frequency and the temperature of each decay (Eq.~\ref{eq:3.27}), and then
$l_{\text{opt}}=m_{\text{at}}K_2/16h$ (Eq.~\ref{K2 et lopt}). Uncertainties are estimated as described in
Sec.~\ref{sec:uncertainties}. Both decays were recorded in the same trap,
with $\bar\omega/2\pi=185\pm9$~Hz and $\langle n\rangle=(1.4\pm0.2)\times10^{13}$~cm$^{-3}$, at
$T=1.8\pm0.1~\mu$K ($\nu=-1$) and $2.0\pm0.1~\mu$K ($\nu=-2$), and at intensities $I=4\pm1$~mW/cm$^2$
($\nu=-1$) and $303\pm17$~mW/cm$^2$ ($\nu=-2$).

\paragraph{$f_{\nu=-1}=-23.99(1)$~MHz}
For the resonance $f_{\nu=-1}=-23.99(1)$~MHz, the fit of the decay gives
$\beta=(1.0\pm0.2)\times10^{-4}$~s$^{-1}$ per atom, i.e.
$K_2=2\sqrt{2}\,V_e\,\beta=(6.3\pm1.6)\times10^{-12}$~cm$^3$/s. The ideal-line relation then gives
$l_{\text{opt}}^{\text{ideal}} = 1.6\pm0.4~a_0$ at $I = 4\pm1~\text{mW/cm}^2$, i.e.
$l_{\text{opt}}^{\text{ideal}}/I = (4.1\pm1.1)\times10^2~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$.
Accounting for the measured width ($\Gamma_{\text{tot}}/(\Gamma_0/2\pi) = 23.0\pm1.2$), the
optical length becomes $l_{\text{opt}} = 37\pm9~a_0$, i.e.
$l_{\text{opt}}/I = (9.2\pm2.4)\times10^3~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$. This lies between the
theoretical value $\xi = 6110~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$ reported by Blatt et
al.~\cite{blatt_systematic_2011} for the corresponding resonance (their $n=-2$ state),
which it exceeds by a factor $1.5\pm0.4$, and their measured value
$15.8(8)\times10^3~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$, a factor $1.7\pm0.5$ above it.
The spectrum of this line (Fig.~\ref{88Sr resonance}, middle), from which its linewidth is
extracted, was recorded with the same MOGlabs laser as the decay $N(t)$, but with the ODT/PA
chopping sequence active (Fig.~\ref{fig:sequence_chopping}), whereas the decay was not. The
chopping itself was checked to have a negligible effect on the atoms, but the PA beam is then
on only half of the time ($20~\mu$s every $40~\mu$s): the time-averaged intensity of this
spectrum is $I/2 = 4\pm1$~mW/cm$^2$ rather than the nominal $8\pm1$~mW/cm$^2$.
Even with this correction, the spectrum and the decay are not consistent for this line: the
loss coefficient deduced from the depth of the spectrum, $(1.2\pm0.3)\times10^{-13}$~cm$^3$/s
at a time-averaged intensity of $4\pm1$~mW/cm$^2$, is a factor $50\pm15$ lower than the one
of the decay once brought to the same intensity and density, whereas the two measurements
differ only by a factor $\simeq1.5$ for the $\nu=-2$ line. We attribute this discrepancy to the
inhomogeneous PA intensity seen by the atoms in the $^{88}\text{Sr}$ measurements, caused by
the misalignment between the PA beam and the atomic cloud discussed at the beginning of
this section: only a fraction of the atoms sees the nominal intensity, and
this fraction can differ between two measurements, so that the effective intensity is poorly
known for each of them.

\paragraph{$f_{\nu=-2}=-222.70(1)$~MHz}
For the resonance $f_{\nu=-2}=-222.70(1)$~MHz, the fit gives
$\beta=(4.0\pm0.3)\times10^{-5}$~s$^{-1}$ per atom, i.e.
$K_2=(2.9\pm0.5)\times10^{-12}$~cm$^3$/s, and the ideal-line relation gives
$l_{\text{opt}}^{\text{ideal}} = 0.77\pm0.12~a_0$ at $I = 303\pm17~\text{mW/cm}^2$, i.e.
$l_{\text{opt}}^{\text{ideal}}/I = 2.5\pm0.4~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$. Accounting for the
measured width ($\Gamma_{\text{tot}}/(\Gamma_0/2\pi) = 22.0\pm0.9$), the optical length becomes
$l_{\text{opt}} = 17\pm3~a_0$, i.e. $l_{\text{opt}}/I = 56\pm10~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$, in
agreement with the value $68(3)~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$ measured by Blatt et
al.~\cite{blatt_systematic_2011} for this line (their $n=-3$ state), and a factor
$1.7\pm0.3$ above the theoretical value $\xi = 32.8~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$.
Our values of $l_{\text{opt}}/I$ are therefore close to those of Blatt et al., but a
quantitative agreement cannot be confirmed: their theoretical values $\xi$ are given without
uncertainty, and the uncertainties quoted on their measured values only come from their fit.
They do not include the uncertainty on the atomic density, which scales as $\bar\omega^3$
with the mean trap frequency and is therefore large; the uncertainties on their measured
values of $l_{\text{opt}}/I$ are thus likely underestimated.



To conclude, the positions of the $^{88}\text{Sr}$ lines are close to the literature values
(within $0.5$~MHz). Once the linewidth is accounted for, the optical lengths also agree with those of Blatt et al.~\cite{blatt_systematic_2011} within a factor $\lesssim2$.
This calibration gives a reliable basis to search for $^{87}\text{Sr}$
molecular lines, provided the following experimental constraints are taken into
account:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parasitic sidebands:} the injection-locked laser has noise components at $\pm$6~MHz and $\pm$12~MHz, which can drive parasitic transitions away from the carrier
    frequency.

    \item \textbf{Laser-induced broadening:} the MOGlabs laser gives molecular lines about twice as broad as the injection-locked laser ($231$ against $122$~kHz).

    \item \textbf{Loss dynamics:} whether the temperature changes or not during the decay, the time evolution of the atom number is well described by Eq.~\ref{eq:final_fit}, obtained by integrating the combined one-
    and two-body loss equation $\frac{dN}{dt} = -\beta N^2 - \Gamma N$.

    \item \textbf{Blind zones:} near $\pm40.48$~MHz from the atomic line, the PDH sidebands of the MOGlabs beam excite the atomic transition, which can hide a molecular line.

    \item \textbf{Extraction of $l_{\text{opt}}$:} the loss coefficient measured at the center of a line is lowered by the broadening. Extracting $l_{\text{opt}}$ requires both this loss coefficient and the measured linewidth (Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected}), ideally measured at the same intensity.
\end{itemize}

The two-body loss coefficient can only be measured accurately if two-body losses dominate
over one-body losses. Having validated our detection, fitting and calibration
procedures on the well-known $^{88}\text{Sr}$ lines, we now turn to the
fermionic isotope $^{87}\text{Sr}$, the main subject of this thesis. Its richer
hyperfine structure and $SU(N)$ symmetry make the spectroscopy more complex
-- see for instance the twelve overlapping $m_F$ resonances identified above. The next
section uses the same method, taking into account the experimental constraints listed here.

\section{$^{87}\text{Sr}$ inelastic collisions}
\label{sec:87Sr_inelastic}

The calibration on the well-known $^{88}\text{Sr}$ isotope in the previous section allowed us
to choose the experimental parameters needed for photoassociation -- exposure times,
laser intensities -- before turning to the fermionic isotope. However, some
signals found while searching for $^{87}\text{Sr}$ molecular lines
were first wrongly interpreted as molecular resonances. The checks
made on these signals, detailed at the beginning of this section, led us to a set of
measurements that test whether a resonance is really molecular.

Molecular line searches on $^{87}\text{Sr}$ are performed only with the MOGlabs source.
To address the fermionic species, the diffraction order of the red optical chain was
changed from $-1$ to $+1$, shifting the addressed frequency by $1.441~\text{GHz}$ -- the
isotopic shift between $^{88}\text{Sr}$ and the $F=11/2$ hyperfine state of
$^{87}\text{Sr}$ (see Fig.~\ref{zoom red chain battement}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Chapitres/Engineering highly entangled system of photoassociated 87Sr atoms/images/Schemas_optiques/zoom_red_chain_beating.pdf}
\caption[Zoom on the part of the red laser chain used for PA]{Zoom on the part of the red laser chain used for PA. For both

All the resonances presented in this section are recorded with a shot-to-shot
normalization, to remove slow drifts of the atom number (due, e.g.,
to temperature or mechanical drifts of the setup): in each pair of cycles, the first atom number is measured
without the PA laser, and the second after exposure to it. This is
repeated throughout the scan, and the plotted signal is the ratio of the
second to the first atom number.

To increase the two-body loss signal, data were taken for several PA
beam waists and atomic densities.

Because of the hyperfine structure of $^{87}\text{Sr}$, about a thousand molecular channels (981) are
expected, as discussed in the theoretical part of this chapter, each line having a natural
linewidth of about $15~\text{kHz}$.
The density of these lines can be estimated from the LeRoy-Bernstein law
(Eq.~\ref{leroy-bernstein}). Near the $^1S_0+{}^3P_1$ asymptote, the binding energies of
the $0_u$ levels of $^{88}\text{Sr}$ ($0.435$, $23.932$, $222.161$ and $1084.093$~MHz
\cite{zelevinsky_narrow_2006}) follow the law for a $C_3/r^3$ potential ($n=3$), with
$E_\nu^{1/6}$ linear in $\nu$ and $H_3\simeq0.78~\text{MHz}^{1/6}$; mass-scaled to
$^{87}\text{Sr}$ ($H_3\propto\mu^{-1/2}$), this gives about three vibrational levels per
molecular channel within the $\simeq380$~MHz explored below the asymptote, the next one
lying around $1$~GHz. Distributing the 981 accessible molecular configurations, each with
$\simeq3.5$ levels, over this range, we expect a dense spectrum of about $3\times10^3$
molecular lines with a mean separation of only $\sim 100~\text{kHz}$ -- a few times larger
than the natural linewidth of a single line, but much smaller than the several-MHz
linewidths we actually observe (Sec.~\ref{raie sr87 }). This estimate assumes that all
channels converge to the same asymptote, and is therefore an upper bound on the line
density.


The rest of this section first analyzes the signals that first
misled us, and the test protocol they led us to set up. It then presents
the molecular lines found for $^{87}\text{Sr}$, with the associated
temperature and atom number evolution. The unexpected shape of these lines leads to a
discussion of their possible technical and physical origins. The section ends with the
tests needed in the future to check the current hypotheses.


\subsection{A protocol to identify molecular lines, learned from misleading signals}
This section presents the study of the loss signal observed at $-6$~MHz from the
$^{87}\text{Sr}$ atomic resonance (Fig.~\ref{6MHz noise red detuned}), which was first
interpreted as a molecular resonance but actually comes from a sideband of the red diode
laser addressing the atomic transition, as shown by the resolved $m_F$ structure observed at
$+6$~MHz (Fig.~\ref{6MHz noise blue detuned}). For these measurements, the atoms were evaporated
for $500$~ms, recompressed, and illuminated for $2000$~ms.

These tests led to a protocol for identifying molecular lines, in which we check the decay model and the temperature evolution.

\subsubsection{Interpretation of the decay $N(t)$}
\label{sec:dNdt_interpretation}

Without the PA laser, the losses are exponential, as
expected for one-body losses due, e.g., to collisions with the
background gas. In the presence of a PA laser, we typically observe a non-exponential decay
(Fig.~\ref{exp decay 6 MHz noise}). However, in this specific case, the non-exponential
decay was observed both for a positive and a negative $6$~MHz detuning with respect to the
atomic resonance. An interpretation is that the loss is then due to a sideband in the
laser spectrum: it heats the gas, and the atoms can be lost from the trap at a rate that
depends on the atom density. Therefore, this process also leads to a non-exponential loss.
As a consequence, each non-exponential loss needs to be carefully checked, which we do by
searching for losses at an equal and opposite detuning from the atomic resonance. The same
problem is pointed out in Ref.~\cite{reschovsky_narrow-line_2018}: since the saturation
intensity of the atomic transition is very small ($I_{\text{sat}}=3~\mu$W/cm$^2$), a small
amount of sideband noise on the PA laser can cause losses easily confused with a PA resonance,
which may explain previously reported lines that the authors could not reproduce.

\subsubsection{Temperature analysis}
\label{sec:temperature_analysis}
Other tests that could indicate inelastic collisions include measuring the temperature
of the gas, which we expect to be hotter near the line center. The coldest atoms sit at
the bottom of the trap, where the density is highest, and are therefore most likely to
photoassociate; once removed, the remaining atoms re-thermalize via elastic collisions,
raising the mean temperature, and hence the volume, of the gas.

Measuring the temperature as a function of illumination time provides a further test:
as an empirical model, not derived here, we describe the heating of the gas by the
saturating exponential
\begin{equation}
    T(t) = T_{\infty} - (T_{\infty} - T_0)\,e^{-\Gamma t}
    \label{eq:T_saturating}
\end{equation}
with $T_0$ and $T_\infty$ the temperature without the PA laser and in the limit of
infinite exposure time, and $\Gamma$ the one-body loss rate extracted from the $N(t)$
fit -- since this rate sets the timescale over which the loss dynamics transition from
the two-body-dominated regime (responsible for the heating) to the one-body-dominated
regime. This shape is not a discriminating test: photon scattering on an atomic
transition also heats the gas, and the resulting evaporation, which depends non-linearly
on $T(t)$ and on the density, also leads to a non-exponential decay and to a curved
$T(t)$. In practice, the temperature variations measured here remain small, and no firm
conclusion on the loss mechanism can be drawn from them.

Figure~\ref{temp 6MHz noise} shows $T(t)$ for the same experiment as
Fig.~\ref{exp decay 6 MHz noise}. The gas heats up during the illumination, but the
shape of $T(t)$ alone does not allow one to distinguish atomic scattering from molecular
photoassociation: the atomic origin of this signal is established by the symmetric
losses observed at equal and opposite detunings (Sec.~\ref{sec:dNdt_interpretation}).

All the temperatures are measured by time of flight, as described in Sec.~\ref{sec:fitting_model}
(Eq.~\ref{eq:deltaT} for their uncertainty).

From the analysis of the diode noise at $-6$~MHz, we conclude that three
complementary tests can help to identify the nature of
the collisions observed on the $^{87}$Sr signals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$dN/dt$ measurement:} the theoretical part of this chapter derives the
    two dynamics of the atomic decay, with different characteristic times, dominated by
    two-body losses at high density and by one-body losses at low density. In practice, comparing this decay in
    the presence and absence of the PA laser was not on its own sufficient to conclude
    on the nature of the collisions.
    \item \textbf{$T(t)$ measurement:} the heating of the gas during the illumination is
    measured, although its shape alone does not discriminate between loss mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{$T(f)$ measurement:} the temperature as a function of the laser detuning
    is shown for each $^{87}$Sr line below; in practice, it only shows negligible variations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Molecular lines on $^{87}$Sr}
\label{raie sr87 }
While searching for molecular resonances on $^{87}$Sr, we found only three resonances,
instead of the 981 expected molecular channels; they are presented in this section.
All the spectra presented in this section have an off-resonant level (baseline) below unity.
Close to the atomic resonance, this is expected, since at high intensity the atomic line is
power broadened. But even far from the atomic resonance ($\simeq 200$~MHz), the baseline of the
normalized data is below unity: we attribute it to atomic losses induced by the PA light, which
still scatters photons on the atomic transition. For $|\Delta|\gg\Omega,\Gamma$, the scattering
rate per atom is $R_{\text{sc}}\simeq\Gamma\,\Omega^2/(4\Delta^2)=\frac{\Gamma}{2}\,s\left(\frac{\Gamma}{2\Delta}\right)^2$,
with $s=I/I_{\text{sat}}=2\Omega^2/\Gamma^2$ and $I_{\text{sat}}\simeq3~\mu$W/cm$^2$. For
$I\simeq19$~W/cm$^2$, i.e. $s\simeq6\times10^6$, and $\Delta/2\pi\simeq-260$~MHz, this gives
$R_{\text{sc}}\sim10$ photons per second and per atom (the Clebsch--Gordan coefficients of the $\pi$
transitions $F=9/2\rightarrow F'=11/2$, averaged over the ten $m_F$ states of the unpolarized gas,
reduce the line strength by a factor $\simeq0.4$). Each photon heats the atom by about two recoil energies
($E_r/k_B\simeq0.23~\mu$K), which heats the gas by $\sim1~\mu$K/s, of the order of the heating
measured on $T(t)$, and leads to losses by evaporation: this is responsible for the baseline below
unity. Since $R_{\text{sc}}\propto I/\Delta^2$, this effect decreases quickly as the laser is
detuned further from the atomic resonance.

\textbf{Line at $-259.01$~MHz}

We now present the spectra in which PA lines were found.

The molecular state closest to the atomic resonance on the red side is found at
$f_1 = -259.01 \pm 0.12$~MHz. It was obtained for an illumination time of
$t=1000$~ms on a recompressed gas of average density
$\langle n\rangle = (7.0\pm1.0)\times10^{12}$~atoms/cm$^3$ and temperature $T=2.38\pm0.08~\mu$K at intensity
$I=19.0\pm1.1$~W/cm$^2$. The PA beam has a waist $w\simeq60~\mu$m; all uncertainties are estimated as described in
Sec.~\ref{sec:uncertainties}. The
Lorentzian fit of the normalized data (each point is the ratio of the atom numbers measured with
and without PA light in two consecutive cycles, see the beginning of this section;
Fig.~\ref{fig:line_259MHz}, top left) gives a
surprisingly large linewidth $\Gamma_{\text{fit}} = 7.4\pm0.9$~MHz, i.e.
$\Gamma_{\text{fit}}/(\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi) \simeq 490$, with a depth of
$40\pm2\%$ of the off-resonant atom number at resonance. The off-resonant level of the
normalized spectrum is itself below unity ($0.91\pm0.02$): away from the molecular line,
the PA light induces an additional loss of $\simeq9\%$ in $1$~s which is not molecular, as
already observed in Fig.~\ref{exp decay 6 MHz noise}: off-resonant photon scattering heats the gas,
which then loses atoms by evaporation. The depth is measured relative to this off-resonant level,
which removes this one-body loss from the two-body signal.

Figure~\ref{fig:line_259MHz} (bottom left) shows the temperature of the cloud measured in the
same experiment, to help confirm the molecular nature of the losses. It only
shows negligible variations across the resonance ($2.43\pm0.07~\mu$K within the half width
against $2.34\pm0.08~\mu$K away from it), comparable to the shot-to-shot scatter, from
which no conclusion can be drawn.

Since the gas heats up during photoassociation and the temperature trace shows large
fluctuations, the decay (Fig.~\ref{fig:line_259MHz}, top right) is fitted with the interpolated-temperature
procedure of Sec.~\ref{decay dynamics}, with four free parameters ($\Gamma$, $K_2$, $N_0$ and a constant offset).
The same procedure is applied to the $-276.83$~MHz line.

The decay was recorded in the same
trap as the spectrum, the gas temperature rising from $2.65\pm0.04$ to $4.70\pm0.13~\mu$K during the decay:
from the measured trap beam powers, the mean trap frequency is
estimated at \textbf{$\bar\omega/2\pi = 224\pm10$ Hz}, giving an average density
\textbf{$\langle n\rangle = (7.0\pm1.0)\times10^{12}$ atoms/cm$^3$}.
The fit yields \textbf{$K_2 = (5.0\pm2.8)\times10^{-13}$ cm$^3$/s} at
\textbf{$I=19.0\pm1.1$ W/cm$^2$}, the same intensity and density as for the spectrum. The
ideal-line relation (Eq.~\ref{eq:K2_peak_ideal}) would give
$l_{\text{opt}}^{\text{ideal}}/I=(6.8\pm3.8)\times10^{-3}$~$a_0$/(W/cm$^2$).
Accounting for the linewidth through Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected}, Method 2 gives
\textbf{$l_{\text{opt}}/I=3.4\pm1.9$ $a_0$/(W/cm$^2$)}. Method 1, using the depth
of the normalized spectrum (Eq.~\ref{eq:K2_from_spectrum}), gives
$K_2^{\text{fit}}(0) = (9.4\pm1.5)\times10^{-14}$~cm$^3$/s, i.e.
\textbf{$l_{\text{opt}}/I=0.63\pm0.13$ $a_0$/(W/cm$^2$)} (including the intensity and
density uncertainties). The two methods differ by a factor $\simeq5$ on $l_{\text{opt}}/I$, but the two loss
coefficients, $(9.4\pm1.5)\times10^{-14}$~cm$^3$/s for the spectrum and
$(5.0\pm2.8)\times10^{-13}$~cm$^3$/s for the decay, remain compatible within $\simeq1.5\sigma$,
owing to the large uncertainty of the decay fit for this line.

The two measurements can also be compared directly, independently of the decay model.
Since the spectrum and the decay share the same intensity and density, the
$(40\pm2)\%$ depth at the line center after $1$~s, i.e.
$K_2\langle n\rangle t=A/(1-A)=0.66\pm0.05$, predicts the same loss after $1$~s on the
decay. The decay, however, also contains the non-molecular PA-induced loss, which is
removed from the spectrum by measuring its depth relative to the off-resonant level. Since the
solution of Eq.~\ref{eq:final_fit} is the product of the one-body factor $e^{-\gamma t}$,
with $\gamma$ the one-body loss rate induced by the off-resonant PA light, and of a
two-body factor, the off-resonant level of the spectrum, $b=e^{-\gamma t}$, cancels in
the depth measured relative to it, and dividing the decay by $b$ isolates the same
two-body factor. The raw
$(49\pm3)\%$ loss read on the decay at $1$~s ($K_2\langle n\rangle t=0.96\pm0.12$) becomes
$(44\pm3)\%$ once divided by the off-resonant level of the spectrum recorded at the same
intensity, i.e. $K_2\langle n\rangle t=0.79\pm0.12$, in agreement with the spectrum within
$\simeq1\sigma$. The larger factor between the two methods on $K_2$ is most likely due to
the decay model: the fitted $K_2$ relies on the interpolated temperature $T(t)$, which rises up to
$4.7~\mu$K and increases $V_e$, whereas the direct comparison at $1$~s does not. 
The gas heats up during the decay (Fig.~\ref{fig:line_259MHz}, bottom right), but, as
discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:temperature_analysis}, the shape of $T(t)$ does not allow one to
identify the heating mechanism.

\textbf{Line at $-276.83$~MHz}

The second line is detected at $f_2 = -276.83\pm0.03$~MHz from the atomic
resonance (Fig.~\ref{fig:line_276MHz}, top left). The gas, prepared at an average
density of $\langle n\rangle = (1.6\pm0.2)\times10^{13}~\text{atoms/cm}^3$ and temperature $T=1.25\pm0.03~\mu$K by evaporation at
the crossing of the reservoir and dimple beams, was illuminated for $1000$~ms at
intensity $I = 5.2\pm0.3$~W/cm$^2$. The measured linewidth,
$\Gamma_{\text{fit}}=5.0\pm0.3$~MHz ($\Gamma_{\text{fit}}/(\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi) \simeq 330$), is again
surprisingly large, and the depth reaches $34.5\pm0.7\%$ of the off-resonant atom number,
whose normalized level is $0.969\pm0.010$. Two points of the spectrum, at $276.3$ and
$281.8$~MHz, lie more than $3\sigma$ away from the fit ($6\sigma$ and $4\sigma$ respectively,
$\sigma$ being the scatter of the residuals). Their absorption images are normal, but the
corresponding shots show an abnormally low temperature (see below): these two points are
therefore not considered and are removed from the spectrum and from the fit. The reduced
$\chi^2$ of the fit is $1.6$, and the uncertainties given here are scaled accordingly.

The temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:line_276MHz}, bottom left) only shows negligible variations
across the resonance ($1.27\pm0.02~\mu$K within the half width against $1.23\pm0.01~\mu$K
away from it), from which no conclusion can be drawn. Three points of this temperature spectrum, at $274.3$,
$276.3$ and $281.8$~MHz, were measured at $T\simeq1.03~\mu$K, i.e. about $7\sigma$ below the
dispersion of the other points ($1.25\pm0.03~\mu$K): they are not considered, and
are removed from Fig.~\ref{fig:line_276MHz}. The shots at $276.3$ and $281.8$~MHz are
the two points removed from the atom number spectrum above.

As for the first line, the atom number decay is fitted with the interpolated-temperature
procedure of Sec.~\ref{decay dynamics}.

For the same trap parameters as for the spectrum (the gas
temperature rising from $2.31\pm0.04$ to $4.72\pm0.18~\mu$K during the decay), the mean trap frequency is
estimated at
\textbf{$\bar\omega/2\pi = 192\pm9$ Hz}, giving an average density
\textbf{$\langle n\rangle = (7.8\pm1.1)\times10^{12}$ atoms/cm$^3$} at the start of the decay.
The fit yields \textbf{$K_2 = (1.1\pm0.2)\times10^{-12}$ cm$^3$/s} at
\textbf{$I=31.0\pm1.8$ W/cm$^2$}. The ideal-line relation (Eq.~\ref{eq:K2_peak_ideal}) would
give $l_{\text{opt}}^{\text{ideal}}/I=(9.2\pm1.5)\times10^{-3}$~$a_0$/(W/cm$^2$).
Accounting for the linewidth measured at $5.2$~W/cm$^2$ through
Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected}, Method 2 gives
\textbf{$l_{\text{opt}}/I=3.1\pm0.5$ $a_0$/(W/cm$^2$)}; since the decay and the
spectrum were recorded at different intensities, this value is a lower bound if the
width increases with intensity (light shift inhomogeneity) or if the line is
light-shifted away from the decay frequency. Method 1, using the depth of the
spectrum at $5.2$~W/cm$^2$, gives $K_2^{\text{fit}}(0) = (3.3\pm0.4)\times10^{-14}$~cm$^3$/s,
i.e. \textbf{$l_{\text{opt}}/I=0.54\pm0.08$ $a_0$/(W/cm$^2$)}. As for the first line, the
factor $\simeq6$ between the two methods is most likely due to the decay model, the
temperature rising up to $4.7~\mu$K during the decay.
Compared directly at $1$~s, the two measurements are compatible.
Assuming $K_2\propto I$, the $(34.5\pm0.7)\%$ depth at the line center after $1$~s at
$5.2$~W/cm$^2$, i.e. $K_2\langle n\rangle t=A/(1-A)=0.53\pm0.02$, corresponds to
$K_2\langle n\rangle t=1.52\pm0.32$ at $31$~W/cm$^2$ and for the density of the decay, that
is a loss of $(60\pm5)\%$ after $1$~s. On the decay, a raw $(74\pm2)\%$ loss is read at the
same time. As for the first line, this raw loss includes the non-molecular PA-induced
loss, measured on the off-resonant level of the spectrum ($0.969\pm0.010$ in $1$~s at
$5.2$~W/cm$^2$). Scaled to $31$~W/cm$^2$ assuming a rate proportional to $I$, this loss
amounts to a factor $0.83\pm0.05$ in $1$~s; once it is removed, the decay gives a
$(69\pm3)\%$ loss, i.e. $K_2\langle n\rangle t=2.2\pm0.3$ (uncertainties from the fit of the
spectrum, from the densities, from the intensities and from the $5\%$ uncertainty on each
atom number of the decay). The two measurements thus
differ by a factor $\simeq1.45$ on $K_2\langle n\rangle t$, i.e. by $\simeq1.5\sigma$, and
remain compatible within $2\sigma$. The residual difference may come from the scaling
between the two measurements, recorded at different intensities, densities and
temperatures ($1.25$ and $2.31~\mu$K). The gas heats up during the decay
(Fig.~\ref{fig:line_276MHz}, bottom right); the saturating-exponential model of Eq.~\ref{eq:T_saturating} does not
reproduce the last three points of the temperature trace, and the shape of $T(t)$ does
not allow one to identify the heating mechanism (Sec.~\ref{sec:temperature_analysis}).

\textbf{Line at $-377.2$~MHz}

The last molecular line is measured at $f_3=-377.2\pm0.5$~MHz, for a
recompressed gas of average density $\langle n\rangle = (7.2\pm1.0)\times10^{12}$~atoms/cm$^3$
and temperature $T=2.38\pm0.04~\mu$K illuminated for $1000$~ms at intensity $I=5.6\pm0.4$~W/cm$^2$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:line_375MHz}, top left). It has a notably small depth ($8.9\pm0.5\%$
loss) and, again, a large linewidth (FWHM) $\Gamma_{\text{fit}}=20.2\pm2.3$~MHz
($\Gamma_{\text{fit}}/(\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi) \simeq 1.3\times10^3$). Each point is the ratio of
the atom number with and without PA light for consecutive shots. The Lorentzian fit is
performed after an iterative $3\sigma$ clipping of the residuals, $\sigma = 0.033$ being
estimated from their median absolute deviation: $24$ of the $651$ points deviate by more
than $3\sigma$ from the fit; they are removed from the data and are not shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:line_375MHz}. The error bars combine in quadrature the uncertainty of the
2D Gaussian fits of the images (diagonal of their covariance matrix) and the relative
RMS fluctuation of the atom number ratio, $3.4\%$, evaluated on the first $100$ points
of the scan, far from the resonance. Because of this shot-to-shot fluctuation, individual
ratios above unity are expected even though the mean ratio cannot exceed one; these points
are therefore kept, since removing them would bias the off-resonant level downwards and
underestimate the depth of the line (removing them lowers the depth from $8.9\%$ to
$7.6\%$). With these error bars, the fit gives a reduced $\chi^2$ of $1.1$.
The temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:line_375MHz}, bottom left) only shows negligible variations
across the resonance ($2.41\pm0.03~\mu$K within the half width against $2.36$ to
$2.39~\mu$K on either side), from which no conclusion can be drawn. Since the temperature
changes less during the decay of this line than for the two other lines, the decay (Fig.~\ref{fig:line_375MHz}, top right) is well
described by Eq.~\ref{eq:final_fit} with a constant
$\beta$.

The decay was recorded in the same trap as for the first line (the gas temperature rising
from $2.28\pm0.03$ to $3.23\pm0.04~\mu$K during the decay): the mean trap
frequency is estimated at \textbf{$\bar\omega/2\pi = 224\pm10$ Hz}, giving an average
density \textbf{$\langle n\rangle = (7.0\pm1.0)\times10^{12}$ atoms/cm$^3$}, close to that of
the spectrum. The fit yields
\textbf{$K_2 = (2.7\pm1.9)\times10^{-14}$ cm$^3$/s} at \textbf{$I=19.0\pm1.1$ W/cm$^2$}. The
ideal-line relation (Eq.~\ref{eq:K2_peak_ideal}) would give
$l_{\text{opt}}^{\text{ideal}}/I=(3.6\pm2.6)\times10^{-4}$~$a_0$/(W/cm$^2$).
Accounting for the linewidth measured at $5.6$~W/cm$^2$ through
Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected}, Method 2 gives
\textbf{$l_{\text{opt}}/I=0.49\pm0.35$ $a_0$/(W/cm$^2$)}, with the same caveat on the
different intensities as for the previous line. Method 1 gives
$K_2^{\text{fit}}(0) = (1.4\pm0.2)\times10^{-14}$~cm$^3$/s at $5.6$~W/cm$^2$, i.e.
\textbf{$l_{\text{opt}}/I=0.84\pm0.17$ $a_0$/(W/cm$^2$)}, consistent with Method 2 within
uncertainties. The two measurements also agree directly once brought to the same
intensity and density: assuming $K_2\propto I$, the $(8.9\pm0.5)\%$ loss of the spectrum
at $5.6$~W/cm$^2$ ($K_2\langle n\rangle t=0.098\pm0.006$) corresponds to
$K_2\langle n\rangle t=0.32\pm0.07$ at $19$~W/cm$^2$ and for the density of the decay,
i.e. a loss of $(24\pm4)\%$ after
$1$~s, in agreement with the $(27\pm5)\%$ raw loss read on the decay (same sources of
uncertainty as for the previous line). Removing the non-molecular PA-induced loss as for
the two other lines (off-resonant level $0.974\pm0.004$ at $5.6$~W/cm$^2$, i.e. a factor
$0.91\pm0.01$ in $1$~s at $19$~W/cm$^2$) gives a $(20\pm6)\%$ loss on the decay, still in
agreement with the spectrum. The
gas heats up moderately during the decay (Fig.~\ref{fig:line_375MHz}, bottom right), from
$2.28$ to $3.23~\mu$K; as for the other lines, the shape of $T(t)$ does not allow one to
identify the heating mechanism.

\textbf{PA lines found on $^{87}$Sr}

These results show several unexpected features:

\begin{itemize}
    \item First, although about $3\times10^3$ molecular lines, separated by $\sim100$~kHz on average, are expected within the $\simeq380$~MHz explored below the asymptote, we did not detect any two-body loss closer to the asymptote than $259.01$~MHz. This is also not consistent with the line positions expected for $^{87}$Sr from the mass scaling of Ref.~\cite{borkowski_mass_2014}.
    \item The linewidth of each line is large: $7.4 \pm 0.9$, $5.0 \pm 0.3$ and $20.2 \pm 2.3$~MHz, while we expect $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi \simeq 2\times \Gamma_{\text{at}}/2\pi \simeq 15$~kHz, i.e. two to three orders of magnitude larger.
    \item As a direct consequence of this broadening, the losses at resonance remain small: the depths of the three lines range from $8.9\pm0.5\%$ to $40\pm2\%$ after $1$~s of illumination (Table~\ref{tab:summary_fits_PA_final}). At fixed $l_{\text{opt}}$, a line $10^2$--$10^3$ times broader than $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ has a peak loss coefficient lowered by the same factor (Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected}): the small depths and the large linewidths are therefore not two independent observations: both reflect a peak loss rate lowered by the broadening.
    \item Once the linewidth is accounted for, the analysis of these data gives optical lengths $l_{\text{opt}}/I \simeq 0.5$--$3.5~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$ (Table~\ref{tab:summary_fits_PA_final}), i.e. one to two orders of magnitude below the $\nu=-2$ line of $^{88}\text{Sr}$ measured in the same setup ($56\pm10~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$), and about two orders of magnitude below the $^{84}$Sr line of similar binding energy ($228(42)~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$ at $228$~MHz \cite{reschovsky_narrow-line_2018}, where $l_{\text{opt}}$ is also corrected for the broadening through a factor $\eta$). They are of the order of the weakest strontium lines, such as the $^{84}$Sr line at $1143$~MHz ($0.95(12)~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$ \cite{reschovsky_narrow-line_2018}), which is suppressed by a node of the ground-state wavefunction; such a node is however unlikely for our lines (Sec.~\ref{sec:physical_hypotheses}).
\end{itemize}


The high intensities needed to observe the $^{87}\text{Sr}$ lines ($5$ to $31$~W/cm$^2$, much higher than for $^{88}\text{Sr}$) come from both a smaller optical length and a peak loss coefficient lowered by the $10^2$--$10^3$ broadening. The ideal-line relation, which neglects the width, underestimates $l_{\text{opt}}$ by this same factor: once the width is accounted for, the main anomaly of these lines is their width rather than their optical length. For comparison, the broadening factor $\eta=\Gamma_{\text{fit}}/(\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/2\pi)$ is only $\simeq1.4$--$1.6$ for the $^{84}$Sr lines of Ref.~\cite{reschovsky_narrow-line_2018} and $\simeq22$ for our $^{88}$Sr lines, against $330$--$1350$ for $^{87}$Sr.
The next subsection discusses possible explanations for these unexpected features.

\subsection{Interpretation of the large linewidth and weak loss rate}
\subsubsection{Possible technical causes}
\label{sec:technical_causes}
The weak loss rate and the large linewidth could first come from technical limitations of the experiment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coupling to higher-energy states by the ODT laser:} the ODT laser at $\lambda = 1075$~nm can induce unwanted photoassociation (PA) or transitions to higher-energy molecular states if it is resonant with a transition starting from the intermediate excited state. To suppress these parasitic multi-photon processes, the ODT and the PA laser are switched on and off in opposite phase (chopping sequence, Fig.~\ref{fig:sequence_chopping}).

            This chopping meets three constraints:
            \begin{enumerate}
                \item \textbf{Suppression of multi-photon processes:} since the ODT ($1075$~nm) and the PA laser ($689$~nm) are never on at the same time, photoassociation paths involving both photons are suppressed.
                \item \textbf{Preservation of the atomic cloud:} the trap is switched off for a time short enough that the cloud does not fall significantly under gravity.
                \item \textbf{AOM response and spectral purity:} the switching period is long enough for the acousto-optic modulators (AOMs) to follow, so the PA laser spectrum is not significantly broadened or distorted.
            \end{enumerate}

        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Chapitres/Engineering highly entangled system of photoassociated 87Sr atoms/images/Figures_pour_theorie/sequence_chopping.pdf}
            \caption[Experimental sequence for ODT and PA laser chopping]{Experimental sequence of the ODT and PA laser chopping used to prevent multi-photon transitions to higher energy levels. The RF signals driving the ODT and Raman AOMs are modulated at $25$~kHz in opposite phase. This ensures that the two lasers do not overlap while keeping the extinction time short enough to avoid atom loss due to gravity.}
            \label{fig:sequence_chopping}
        \end{figure}

        This possibility was ruled out by tests on $^{88}\text{Sr}$ and $^{87}\text{Sr}$ molecular lines, which did not change with the chopping.
    \item \textbf{Noise of the MOGlabs laser:} on the $^{88}$Sr molecular lines, this laser gave widths about 20 times larger than the expected $\simeq15$~kHz, which is not enough to explain the MHz linewidths observed on $^{87}$Sr.
    \item \textbf{Inhomogeneous trap:} the atoms could see different trap potentials if they are not all cold enough to stay at the center of the trap.
    \item \textbf{Inhomogeneous light shift:} the intensities used on $^{87}\text{Sr}$ ($5$--$31$~W/cm$^2$) are much higher than on $^{88}\text{Sr}$. The PA beam induces a light shift of the molecular state, which reaches $\sim 200$~kHz for $0.8$~W/cm$^2$ on the $\nu=-1$ state of $^{88}\text{Sr}$ ($-251$~kHz per W/cm$^2$ \cite{blatt_measurement_2011}). With this coefficient, the waist $w\simeq60~\mu$m of the PA beam and a cloud of rms size $\sigma\simeq11~\mu$m, the intensity varies by $\simeq25\%$ over $\pm2\sigma$, which spreads the resonance over $\simeq1$~MHz at $19$~W/cm$^2$ and $\simeq0.3$~MHz at $5.6$~W/cm$^2$. Coefficients about $10$ to $60$ times larger would be needed to explain the observed widths of $5$ to $20$~MHz, and the broadest line ($20$~MHz) was measured at the lowest intensity. Such a broadening scales with the intensity and could be tested by measuring the width of a line at several intensities.
    \item \textbf{Thermal width:} a high gas temperature could broaden the lines, but the thermal width at similar temperatures ($\simeq12$--$13$~kHz, Tables~\ref{tab:88Sr-lines-summary} and~\ref{tab:comparaison_lasers}) is negligible compared
    with the MHz linewidths observed.
\end{itemize}

Since these technical causes do not explain the observations, we now consider physical explanations.

\subsubsection{Physical hypotheses for the weak loss rate and large linewidth}
\label{sec:physical_hypotheses}
As discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:determining_lopt}, the measured peak loss coefficient combines two factors: the optical length of the line, set by the coupling between the colliding pair and the molecular state, and the ratio $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ between the radiative and the total width of the line. We first discuss the effects that can lower the optical length without broadening the line, then those that can broaden it.

\textbf{Effects lowering the optical length.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Node of the ground-state wavefunction:} if the Condon radius of a
    transition coincides with a node of the ground-state scattering wavefunction, the
    Franck--Condon factor vanishes and the corresponding line cannot be excited. This is
    unlikely here: the Condon radius of our lines (roughly $60$--$70~a_0$, scaling as $E_b^{-1/3}$
    the Condon radius $R_c\simeq150~a_0$ of the $^{84}$Sr line at $23$~MHz
    \cite{reschovsky_narrow-line_2018}) is close to that of the $^{84}$Sr line at $228$~MHz, whose
    optical length is not suppressed ($228(42)~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$ \cite{reschovsky_narrow-line_2018}).
    Since the scattering lengths of the two isotopes are close ($a_{87}=96~a_0$
    \cite{hill_photoassociative_2021}, $a_{84}=123~a_0$ \cite{reschovsky_narrow-line_2018}), their
    ground-state wavefunctions are similar at this distance, although only a calculation of the
    Franck--Condon factors could fully rule out a node. Moreover, this mechanism reduces
    $l_{\text{opt}}$ through the Franck--Condon factor but does not broaden the line: it cannot
    explain the observed linewidths.
    \item \textbf{Quantum statistics:} quantum statistics alone lowers the optical length of $^{87}\text{Sr}$ by a factor
    $g^{(2)}_f/g^{(2)}_b\simeq0.9/2\simeq0.45$ with respect to the bosonic $^{88}\text{Sr}$
    \cite{hill_photoassociative_2021}, where $g^{(2)}$ is the pair correlation function at short
    distance. For thermal bosons, $g^{(2)}_b=2$. In the unpolarized gas, two atoms drawn at random
    are in the same $m_F$ state with probability $1/N$, with $N=10$: they are then identical fermions,
    and the Pauli principle gives $g^{(2)}=0$. With probability $1-1/N$, they are in different $m_F$
    states and behave as distinguishable particles, with $g^{(2)}=1$. Hence
    $g^{(2)}_f=(1/N)\times0+(1-1/N)\times1=1-1/N=0.9$, and not simply $1$. An expected value of
    $l_{\text{opt}}/I$ cannot however be estimated from this factor, since the Franck-Condon factor of
    each line differs between the two isotopes.
    \item \textbf{Spin selection:} spin selection further lowers the measured loss coefficient. In the unpolarized gas, each atom is in one of the ten $m_F$ states of $F=9/2$ with probability $1/10$. Two identical fermions colliding in the s-wave must be in an antisymmetric spin state, $(|m_{F,1}m_{F,2}\rangle-|m_{F,2}m_{F,1}\rangle)/\sqrt2$ with $m_{F,1}\neq m_{F,2}$: there are $45$ such states, each with a weight $1/10\times1/10$, which gives the factor $45/100\simeq0.45$ above. For $\pi$ light, a resolved molecular line has a well-defined total projection $T=m_{F,1}+m_{F,2}$ and can only be reached from the antisymmetric states with this projection. Among the colliding pairs, the loss coefficient is therefore lowered by the factor
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{1}{45}\sum_{\substack{m_{F,1}<m_{F,2}\\ m_{F,1}+m_{F,2}=T}} \big|C_{m_{F,1}m_{F,2}}\big|^2 \;\leq\; \frac{1}{45}\left\lfloor\frac{10-|T|}{2}\right\rfloor,
    \end{equation}
    where $C_{m_{F,1}m_{F,2}}$ is the Clebsch--Gordan coefficient of the coupling between the pair spin state and the molecular state, and $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor$ the integer part. For $T=-8$, only the pair $\{-9/2,-7/2\}$ contributes, i.e. at most $1/45$; near $T=0$, five pairs contribute, i.e. at most $1/9$. This gives a reduction by one to two orders of magnitude depending on $T$, which could explain the small values of $l_{\text{opt}}$; the exact factor depends on the hyperfine structure of each molecular state, which is not known here. This factor comes in addition to the quantum-statistics factor above.
\end{itemize}
Quantum statistics and spin selection together can explain optical lengths one to two orders of magnitude below those of the bosonic isotopes, but none of these effects explains the observed linewidths.

\textbf{Effects broadening the lines.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coupling to higher molecular potentials via potential crossings:} molecular
    potentials correlated to higher atomic asymptotes, such as $^1S_0+{}^1D_2$, can cross the
    potentials of the intercombination line. Such an avoided crossing between the $0_u^+$
    potentials of the $^1S_0+{}^3P_1$ and $^1S_0+{}^1D_2$ asymptotes has been shown to strongly
    perturb the $0_u^+$ levels of $^{84}\text{Sr}$ \cite{borkowski_mass_2014,reschovsky_narrow-line_2018}.
    A molecular level lying close to such a crossing acquires part of the character of the
    other state, which shifts it and can open additional decay channels. 
    Artlce \cite{borkowski_mass_2014} shows that the 84 isotope is coupled to an excited molecular 
    state which induces avoided crossing, but it does not predicts this effect on the $^{87}Sr$.
    We have no
    quantitative estimate of this effect for the $^{87}\text{Sr}$ levels observed here.
    \item \textbf{Predissociation:} a small number of broad PA lines with weak losses, as for $^{87}$Sr, was also observed in Ref.~\cite{franzen_intercombination_2023} for $^{87}$Rb$^{170}$Yb
        molecules photoassociated near the $^1S_0\rightarrow{}^3P_1$ intercombination line of Yb.
        In a search over binding energies from $0.1$ to $11$~GHz, only a single pair of lines was detected, around $3.1$~GHz. The loss coefficient of the strongest line is only $K_{\text{PA}}\simeq5\times10^{-14}$~cm$^3$/s at $I\simeq40$~W/cm$^2$,
        of the same order as our $K_2$ values (Table~\ref{tab:summary_fits_PA_final}). These lines are also broad: their width extrapolated to zero magnetic field is $3$~MHz (FWHM), about $17$ times the atomic linewidth of Yb ($2\pi\times181$~kHz); it increases with the magnetic field and does not decrease when the PA intensity is lowered. Since $K_{\text{PA}}$ is measured at the line center without accounting for this width, the corresponding line strength is underestimated by the same factor (Sec.~\ref{sec:determining_lopt}).

        The authors give two interpretations. The first is a strong radiative decay to bound states of the electronic ground state, proposed for RbSr; for RbYb, they estimate that the transition dipole moment of the excited state increases by less than $10\%$ of the atomic one, which makes this mechanism unlikely.

        The second interpretation is a non-radiative decay within the $^3P$ manifold, for example a predissociation towards the $^3P_0$ state, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{predissociation}. Two free atoms in potential A are photoassociated to a vibrational state of a molecular potential B. A
        potential C crosses potential B above its dissociation limit, which
        couples the two potentials \cite{mukherjee_feshbach_2022}. For $^{87}$Sr, potential C would be one of the potentials associated with the hyperfine structure of the $^3P_0$ states. The bound pair can then decay into the
        continuum of potential C. This adds a decay channel to the excited molecular state: its lifetime
        is shortened, and the molecular line is broadened beyond its radiative width $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$ (the total width $\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ exceeds $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$).
        Following Fermi's golden rule, this additional width is proportional to the density of states of
        the continuum of potential C at the energy of the bound level, and to the square of the coupling
        between the two potentials. Since the pair is lost in both decay channels, predissociation does not
        change $l_{\text{opt}}$, which is defined with respect to the radiative width $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$, but
        lowers the peak loss coefficient by the ratio $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}/\Gamma_{\text{mol}}$ (Eq.~\ref{K2 et lopt}),
        the area under the line remaining unchanged (Eq.~\ref{eq:lopt_corrected}). A broad line with a weak peak loss rate is therefore precisely the signature expected from strong predissociation. Contrary to a light shift inhomogeneity, this broadening does not depend on the PA intensity. In Ref.~\cite{franzen_intercombination_2023}, such a decay could broaden most of the lines, especially in a magnetic field, enough to make them unobservable, which would explain why a single pair of lines was found. Contrary to RbYb, however, the width of our $^{87}$Sr lines does not change with the magnetic field (Sec.~\ref{sec:tests_future}). 

        In Fig.~\ref{fig:pa-ytterbium-potentials}, the linewidths do not seem to be affected by predissociation. We expected that, as for Yb,
        photoassociation below the lowest hyperfine asymptote would avoid this effect, but our $^{87}\text{Sr}$ data suggest the contrary: we do not know why
        $^{87}\text{Sr}$ would be affected by this process while Yb, which also has a hyperfine structure, is not.
        
        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering
            \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Chapitres/Engineering highly entangled system of photoassociated 87Sr atoms/images/Figures_pour_theorie/predissociation.pdf}
            \caption[Scheme of predissociation]{Scheme of predissociation. It occurs when the dissociation limit of an excited molecular potential
        C lies below the bound state $\nu_B$ of the excited potential B. The $\nu_B$ state is then coupled to the continuum of potential
        C, which broadens the molecular line beyond $\Gamma_{\text{rad}}$.}
            \label{predissociation}
        \end{figure}

In $^{87}$Sr, the hyperfine structure plays both roles. It shares the line strength among many
spin components, which lowers the optical length of each resolved line (spin selection above),
and it provides lower-lying channels, correlated to other hyperfine asymptotes, into which the
bound pair can predissociate, which broadens the lines.
\end{itemize}
Together with an inhomogeneous light shift (Sec.~\ref{sec:technical_causes}), predissociation is the most likely explanation of the broad linewidth of the molecular lines, and hence of their weak loss rate at the line center. The two can be distinguished by measuring the width of a line as a function of the PA intensity: a light shift broadening grows with the intensity, whereas predissociation does not. 

This section ends with the experiments that could clarify this point.

\subsubsection{Tests to be done in the future}
\label{sec:tests_future}
Some tests have already been made on the lines we found, and others remain to be done:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Changing the polarization of the PA laser could change the coupling strength with the atomic continuum. This test showed no significant change of the line.
    \item Changing the magnetic field could change the energy difference between the hyperfine states and so decrease the density of states. This was also tested: neither the width nor the position of the lines
    changed significantly, and no individual molecular lines were resolved.
    \item Measuring the width of a given line at several PA intensities would discriminate an intensity-dependent broadening (inhomogeneous light shift) from an intensity-independent one (predissociation).
    \item Recording the spectrum and the atom number decay at the same intensity would allow a direct comparison of the two methods of Sec.~\ref{sec:determining_lopt} for every line.
    \item Optically pumping the atoms to reduce the size of the Hilbert space would reduce the number of atomic potentials and so the probability of predissociation. Experiments are
    in progress to test this hypothesis. A magnetic field switch has already been implemented in the experiment to populate the extremal $m_F$ states. So far, the measurements of $T(t)$ and $T(f)$ have not given any information on the loss mechanism.
\end{itemize}


        

To conclude, this chapter first introduced the theory of the PA process (scattering length, optical length, two-body loss dynamics).
It then presented PA measurements on the intercombination line of $^{88}$Sr, used as a reference to characterize the experimental procedure. It ends with the measurement of molecular lines in an
unpolarized gas of $^{87}$Sr, with linewidths two to three orders of magnitude larger than the radiative width, which account for their weak loss rate at the line center. Once this broadening is taken into account, the optical lengths reach $l_{\text{opt}}/I \sim 0.5$--$3.5~a_0/(\text{W/cm}^2)$, one to two orders of magnitude below the bosonic lines of similar binding energy, which can be explained by quantum statistics and spin selection. The main anomaly of these lines is therefore their width. Predissociation and an inhomogeneous light shift remain possible explanations; measuring the width of a line as a function of the PA intensity would distinguish them.
